\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ===============================================================

Hydrogen isotope transport controls fuel retention, permeation losses and
tritium accountancy in fusion systems. In liquid-breeder concepts the tritium
path crosses at least one metal/liquid interface before it reaches an extraction
or release point, so the condition imposed at that interface enters every
quantity a designer needs to predict.

Macroscopic transport codes such as \FESTIM{} \cite{dark_festim_2026},
\TMAP{}8 \cite{simon_moose-based_2025} and \MHIMS{}
\cite{hodille_macroscopic_2015} close such interfaces with local thermodynamic
equilibrium (LTE), that is, with continuity of the chemical potential of the
transported species. In practice this becomes an algebraic constraint of
Dirichlet type, imposed per species, relating the two interfacial concentrations
\cite{ogorodnikova_model_2002,delaporte-mathurin_influence_2021}: it reads
$c_A/K_{S,A} = c_B/K_{S,B}$ where hydrogen dissolves dissociatively on both
sides, and $c_B = K_{H,B}\,(c_A/K_{S,A})^2$ where it dissolves dissociatively on
one side and molecularly on the other.

Three assumptions are hidden in that closure, and they are rarely stated
together. (H1) is that interfacial equilibration is fast compared with bulk
transport. (H2) is that a \emph{single} exchange pathway connects the two sides.
(H3) is that the identity of the carrier species on each side is known a priori
and fixed, so that one solubility law applies throughout.

Of the three, (H1) is the one usually scrutinised, and it is known to fail.
Molecular dynamics at the Be/BeO interface shows that LTE is not reached even
at \qty{1500}{\kelvin} \cite{hodille_molecular_2022}, and a combined
density-functional and rate-equation treatment of W/Cu shows that the steady
state reached at such an interface is not the thermodynamic equilibrium state
whenever a net flux is carried \cite{silva-solis_hydrogen_2026}. Both generalise
the closure along interfacial storage and two-step kinetics, by giving the
interfacial plane a state of its own. The generalisation we pursue runs in a
different direction, along the number of channels and the identity of the
carriers, and the two compose.

\KEY{(H2) and (H3) have received almost no attention, and we argue that they are
the more consequential failures.} The system that motivates the argument is a
metal in contact with a fluoride melt, where neither assumption survives contact
with the chemistry.

Hydrogen leaving a metal lattice into a molten fluoride has more than one
chemical fate. It may recombine to $\mathrm{H_2}$ and dissolve physically,
obeying Henry's law with a low solubility and a high mobility, or it may be
oxidised by the melt to HF and dissolve chemically, with a much higher
solubility, corrosive behaviour and a strong dependence on the redox state of
the salt. The branching between the two is kinetic, and it moves with that redox
state \cite{lam_impact_2021,carotti_electrochemical_2021}. Assumption (H2)
asserts that one of these pathways may be ignored, and the chemistry does not
say which.

The ambiguity is not hypothetical. Measuring tritium permeation through a nickel
membrane into FLiBe, Calderoni et al.\ \cite{calderoni_measurement_2008}
concluded that tritium absorbed atomically in the nickel does \emph{not} simply
recombine at the Ni/FLiBe interface, and could not distinguish transport as T
bound to $\mathrm{BeF_4^-}$ from transport as HT, or from a mixture of the two.
Nearly two decades later the speciation at that interface is still an open
question, and reported FLiBe transport properties for $\mathrm{H_2}$ and HF, and
a fortiori for their isotopologues, still span orders of magnitude
\cite{libra_2022}.

The ambiguity has already contaminated the data record, because \emph{a
solubility constant has no law-independent units}. Sieverts-type dissolution
gives \unit{\mole\per\cubic\metre\pascal\tothe{-1/2}} and Henry-type gives
\unit{\mole\per\cubic\metre\per\pascal}, and the two cannot be interconverted by
any fixed factor, so reporting a number presupposes the speciation that is
itself the quantity in question. Section~\ref{sec:units} takes the point up. We
treat it as a motivating symptom and not as a criticism of any measurement: it
is what assumption (H3) looks like when it fails in practice.

With more than one isotope the difficulty becomes structural. Two mobile atomic
species in the metal, H and T, feed five carriers in the salt, $\mathrm{H_2}$,
HT, $\mathrm{T_2}$, HF and TF. The map between them is quadratic and
non-diagonal, since $c_\mathrm{HT}$ depends on the \emph{product} of the two
metal-side concentrations, and no per-species condition on the continuity of a
chemical potential can express such a map. LTE here is not inaccurate, it is
ill-posed.

Every ingredient of a kinetic interface condition is standard somewhere.
Finite-rate surface kinetics has closed gas/solid interfaces for four decades
\cite{ali-khan_rate_1978,pick_model_1985}; detailed balance is the usual
constraint tying forward and reverse constants in a surface reaction network
\cite{mhadeshwar_thermodynamic_2003,gorban_extended_2011}; and the geochemistry
community abandoned local equilibrium for transport-coupled interfacial
chemistry three decades ago, on the same grounds, deciding when it may
nonetheless be used with a Damk\"ohler number that compares the rate of the
interfacial reaction with the rate at which transport supplies it
\cite{knapp_spatial_1989,steefel_coupled_1994}. What is
missing is the assembly: no macroscopic hydrogen transport code closes a
condensed/condensed interface with competing reaction channels and multiple
isotopologues.

This paper supplies that assembly. We formulate a general kinetic interface
framework in which the algebraic constraint is replaced by reversible reaction
channels obeying mass action, with detailed balance fixing the ratio of each
pair of rate constants from the same thermodynamic data that parameterise LTE,
and we implement it in \FESTIM{} \cite{dark_festim_2026}. We recover LTE
analytically as the fast-kinetics limit of a single channel, in both its
Sieverts/Sieverts and Sieverts/Henry forms, so that what we propose is a strict
generalisation of LTE, not a competing model. We then derive two dimensionless
groups that delimit its validity, a Damk\"ohler number within a channel and a
branching ratio between channels. Finally, we apply the framework to the
\HYPERION{} Ni/FLiBe/gas experiment \cite{hyperion_facility}, in single-isotope
and in H/T modes.

Interfaces are treated throughout as reactive surfaces without their own stored
inventory. Intermediate trapped states localised on the interface, and the
codimension-1 formulation needed to carry an interfacial ODE alongside the bulk
equations, are the subject of a companion paper \cite{festim_codim1_inprep}, as
are the Be/BeO and W/Cu interface-trapping cases. The two descriptions are
nested, and App.~\ref{app:limits} gives the limit in which the one used here is
recovered from the other.

\section{Interface conditions for hydrogen transport}
\label{sec:models}
% ===============================================================

Throughout this section we consider two subdomains $\Omega_A$ and $\Omega_B$
sharing an interface $\Gamma$. In each subdomain the transport of a dissolved
species obeys the usual diffusion equation
$\partial_t c = \nabla\!\cdot\!(D\nabla c) + S$ solved by \FESTIM{}
\cite{dark_festim_2026}; trapping is omitted for clarity and plays no part in
the arguments that follow. What distinguishes the models discussed below is
solely how the two bulk problems are coupled across $\Gamma$.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Local thermodynamic equilibrium and its assumptions}
\label{sec:lte}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE) is the statement that the chemical
potential of dissolved hydrogen is continuous across $\Gamma$. Since the
relation between chemical potential and concentration depends on the
dissolution law obeyed on each side, the resulting algebraic condition takes a
different form depending on which pair of laws applies. For two materials in
which hydrogen dissolves dissociatively (Sieverts' law on both sides) it reads

\begin{equation}
    \frac{c_A\big|_\Gamma}{K_{S,A}(T)}
    = \frac{c_B\big|_\Gamma}{K_{S,B}(T)}
    \qquad \text{(Sieverts / Sieverts)}
    \label{eq:lte_ss}
\end{equation}

whereas for a solid in contact with a liquid in which hydrogen dissolves
molecularly (Henry's law) it becomes

\begin{equation}
    c_B\big|_\Gamma
    = K_{H,B}(T)\left(\frac{c_A\big|_\Gamma}{K_{S,A}(T)}\right)^{2}
    \qquad \text{(Sieverts / Henry)} .
    \label{eq:lte_sh}
\end{equation}

Both conditions are \emph{algebraic}: they fix a ratio, or a power law, between
the two interfacial concentrations independently of the flux the interface is
carrying. Three consequences follow, and they are properties of the closure,
not of the physical system it is applied to. First, the interface has
no timescale of its own: it responds instantaneously to any change in the
adjacent bulk fields. Second, it offers no resistance, so $\Gamma$ can never be
the rate-limiting step of a permeation problem. Third, the exponent
distinguishing Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss}) from Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}), and hence
the identity of the carrier species on the $B$ side, must be chosen before the
simulation is run.

There is also a formal difficulty, following
Silva-Sol\'is et al.\ \cite{silva-solis_hydrogen_2026}. Thermodynamic
equilibrium requires every microscopic forward rate to be balanced by its
reverse, which implies \emph{zero} net flux across $\Gamma$. A permeation
experiment, however, carries a nonzero net flux by construction. Imposing LTE
at a permeating interface is therefore always an approximation; the only
question is how large the error is, and Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler} makes that
question quantitative.

Two notions are routinely conflated and are kept distinct throughout.
A \emph{steady state} satisfies $\partial_t c = 0$ with
$\Gamma_\mathrm{in} = \Gamma_\mathrm{out} \neq 0$, whereas \emph{equilibrium}
satisfies $\partial_t c = 0$ with
$\Gamma_\mathrm{in} = \Gamma_\mathrm{out} = 0$. Equilibrium is a steady state;
the converse is false, and it is precisely the difference between the two that
LTE discards.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{A general kinetic interface framework}
\label{sec:general}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

We replace the algebraic constraint by a set of reversible reaction channels
living on $\Gamma$. Let $X_i$ denote the mobile atomic species on side $A$
($i \in \{\mathrm{H,D,T}\}$) and $Y_\alpha$ the carriers on side $B$
($\alpha \in \{\mathrm{H_2, HT, T_2, HF, TF},\dots\}$). A channel $r$ is written

\begin{equation}
    \sum_i \nu_{ir} X_i + \sum_\beta \lambda_{\beta r} Z_\beta
    \;\rightleftharpoons\; \sum_\alpha \mu_{\alpha r} Y_\alpha ,
    \label{eq:channel}
\end{equation}

where the $Z_\beta$ are non-hydrogenic constituents entering the stoichiometry
(the fluoride ion and the oxidising or reducing half of the salt redox buffer,
in the application of Sec.~\ref{sec:model3}). We represent them by activities
$a_\beta$ instead of transported fields. Each channel proceeds at a net rate
given by mass action,

\begin{equation}
    w_r = k_r^{+}(T)\prod_i \left(\cm{i}\big|_\Gamma\right)^{\nu_{ir}}
                     \prod_\beta a_\beta^{\lambda_{\beta r}}
        - k_r^{-}(T)\prod_\alpha \left(\cs{\alpha}\big|_\Gamma\right)^{\mu_{\alpha r}} ,
    \label{eq:mass_action}
\end{equation}

expressed per unit interfacial area, so that $w_r$ has units of
\unit{\mole\per\square\metre\per\second} and the units of $k_r^{\pm}$ depend on
the order of the channel: an exchange velocity in \unit{\metre\per\second} for
a first-order channel, \unit{\metre\tothe{4}\per\mole\per\second} for a
second-order one. The interface conditions are then the statement that the net
atomic flux of isotope $i$ leaving $A$, and the production of carrier $\alpha$
into $B$, are sums over all channels, $\mathbf{n}$ denoting throughout the
outward normal of the subdomain whose equation is being written:

\begin{align}
    -D^{\mathrm{m}}_i \nabla \cm{i}\cdot \mathbf{n}\big|_\Gamma
        &= \sum_r \nu_{ir}\, w_r
    \label{eq:bc_metal}\\
     D^{\mathrm{s}}_\alpha \nabla \cs{\alpha}\cdot \mathbf{n}\big|_\Gamma
        &= \sum_r \mu_{\alpha r}\, w_r .
    \label{eq:bc_salt}
\end{align}

Conservation of atoms across $\Gamma$ is not an additional requirement but a
condition on the stoichiometric coefficients. Writing $n_{i\alpha}$ for the
number of atoms of isotope $i$ carried by species $\alpha$, every channel must
satisfy

\begin{equation}
    \nu_{ir} = \sum_\alpha \mu_{\alpha r}\, n_{i\alpha}
    \qquad \text{for each isotope } i ,
    \label{eq:atom_balance}
\end{equation}

so that the atomic flux removed from $A$ by Eq.~(\ref{eq:bc_metal}) is exactly
the atomic flux delivered to $B$ by Eq.~(\ref{eq:bc_salt}). For the
recombination channel of Sec.~\ref{sec:model2}, for instance,
$\nu_\mathrm{H} = 2$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{H_2}} = 1$ with
$n_{\mathrm{H,H_2}} = 2$.

The forward and reverse rate constants of each channel are not independent.
Detailed balance requires

\begin{equation}
    \frac{k_r^{+}(T)}{k_r^{-}(T)} = K_r(T) ,
    \label{eq:detailed_balance_general}
\end{equation}

with $K_r$ the equilibrium constant of that channel, obtained from the
solubilities of the participating species and the relevant formation free
energies. Thermodynamics therefore fixes the \emph{ratio} of the rate
constants, while their \emph{magnitude}, that is, how fast the interface
equilibrates, must come from atomistics or from experiment. The framework is
therefore a strict generalisation of LTE: the same thermodynamic data that
parameterise Eqs.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss}) and~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}) parameterise
Eq.~(\ref{eq:mass_action}), and the additional information supplied is exactly
the information LTE assumes away.
Equation~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_general}) constrains whoever writes the model
and cannot be enforced by the discretisation
\cite{mhadeshwar_thermodynamic_2003,mhadeshwar_thermodynamically_2005,gorban_extended_2011};
Sec.~\ref{sec:params} returns to how the ratios are obtained in practice.
Treating $\Gamma$ as a thermodynamic subsystem with its own entropy production
and its own finite transport coefficients is the standard move in the
non-equilibrium thermodynamics of heterogeneous systems
\cite{kjelstrup_non-equilibrium_2019}, and Eq.~(\ref{eq:mass_action}) is that
idea written in mass-action form.

The framework is agnostic to the nature of the two sides: the gas/solid
dissociation--recombination boundary condition already available in \FESTIM{}
\cite{ali-khan_rate_1978,pick_model_1985,hodille_kinetic_2020,kulagin_kinetic_2024}
is the special case in which $A$ is a gas phase. The models below differ from
one another only in their stoichiometry and in the number of channels they
carry, and Fig.~\ref{fig:closures} collects them on the same geometry, together
with the LTE closure they generalise.

\subsection{Model 1: first-order exchange}
\label{sec:model1}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The simplest case is a single channel $X_A \rightleftharpoons X_B$ in which an
atom crosses the interface without changing chemical identity, as at a
metal/metal interface (Fig.~\ref{fig:closures}b).
Equation~(\ref{eq:mass_action}) then reduces to a
Robin-type flux condition,

\begin{equation}
    \phi = k^{+} c_A\big|_\Gamma - k^{-} c_B\big|_\Gamma ,
    \label{eq:model1_flux}
\end{equation}

with both rate constants an exchange velocity in \unit{\metre\per\second},
closed by mass conservation, $-D_A \,\partial_n c_A = D_B\, \partial_n c_B =
\phi$. Setting $\phi = 0$ and requiring the resulting concentration ratio to
coincide with Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss}) fixes

\begin{equation}
    \frac{k^{+}}{k^{-}} = \frac{K_{S,B}(T)}{K_{S,A}(T)} ,
    \label{eq:detailed_balance_1}
\end{equation}

which is Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_general}) for this channel. Taking
$k^{+}, k^{-} \rightarrow \infty$ at fixed ratio recovers
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss}) exactly, since a finite flux then requires the bracket in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:model1_flux}) to vanish. The derivation is given in
App.~\ref{app:limits}; the correction at finite rate constants is
$\mathcal{O}(\phi/k^{+}c)$ and is quantified in Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler}.

An interfacial rate constant in series with a bulk diffusive resistance is
familiar in the solid state, underlying the linear term of the linear-parabolic
oxidation law \cite{deal_general_1965}, the critical thickness separating
reaction-controlled from diffusion-controlled growth in binary diffusion couples
\cite{gosele_growth_1982,cui_interface-reaction_2013} and, for heat, the Kapitza
resistance \cite{swartz_thermal_1989}. At a metal/metal interface LTE is
normally safe. Interstitial diffusion is itself a first-order exchange between
adjacent planes of sites, so Fick's law is the special case
$k^{+} = D/\lambda$ with $\lambda$ the jump distance, and unless the interfacial
plane is structurally distinct the Damk\"ohler number of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:damkohler}) below is $L/\lambda \sim 10^{6}$ to $10^{7}$ for a
millimetre slab.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Model 2: recombination into a molecular carrier}
\label{sec:model2}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The second case retains a single channel but changes the chemical identity of
the transported species, $2\,X_A \rightleftharpoons Y_{A_2}$: atomic hydrogen
dissolved in the metal recombines into a molecule that dissolves physically in
the liquid (Fig.~\ref{fig:closures}c). It is the metal/molten-salt analogue of
surface recombination, and its net rate is

\begin{equation}
    w_{\mathrm{rec}} = k_{\mathrm{r}}^{+}\left(\cm{\mathrm{H}}\big|_\Gamma\right)^{2}
                     - k_{\mathrm{r}}^{-}\,\cs{\mathrm{H_2}}\big|_\Gamma ,
    \label{eq:model2_rate}
\end{equation}

with interface conditions
$-D^{\mathrm{m}}\partial_n \cm{\mathrm{H}} = 2 w_{\mathrm{rec}}$ and
$D^{\mathrm{s}}\partial_n \cs{\mathrm{H_2}} = w_{\mathrm{rec}}$, the factor two
being the stoichiometry of Eq.~(\ref{eq:atom_balance}). Imposing
$w_{\mathrm{rec}} = 0$ together with Sieverts' law in the metal
($c^{\mathrm{m}} = K_S\sqrt{P}$) and Henry's law in the liquid
($c^{\mathrm{s}} = K_H P$) gives

\begin{equation}
    \frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}^{+}}{k_{\mathrm{r}}^{-}}
    = \frac{K_{H}(T)}{K_{S}(T)^{2}} .
    \label{eq:detailed_balance_2}
\end{equation}

Substituting Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_2}) back into
$w_{\mathrm{rec}} = 0$ returns precisely the Sieverts/Henry condition,
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}). The mixed-law interface currently available in
\FESTIM{}~v2 \cite{dark_festim_2026} is therefore the fast-kinetics limit of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}). Stated plainly: the two interface laws of
Sec.~\ref{sec:lte} are not two separate physical models to be selected between,
but the fast limits of two different channels of the same framework. Which one
applies is a question about chemistry, not about which option to set in an
input file.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Model 3: competing channels}
\label{sec:model3}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Nothing in Sec.~\ref{sec:general} restricts the interface to a single channel,
and at a metal/fluoride-melt interface there is no reason to expect one.
Hydrogen leaving the metal may recombine and dissolve molecularly, or it may be
oxidised by the melt and dissolve as HF, which is far more soluble, corrosive,
and redox dependent. Competing interfacial half-reactions of different order
setting the operating point is the ordinary situation in the corrosion of metals
by molten fluorides \cite{chan_deciphering_2023}. We therefore let two channels
operate on the same interface (Fig.~\ref{fig:closures}d),
\begin{align*}
    \text{R:}&\quad 2\,\mathrm{H(m)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H_2(s)} \\
    \text{F:}&\quad \mathrm{H(m)} + \mathrm{F^-(s)} + h^{+}
                    \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{HF(s)} ,
\end{align*}
where $h^{+}$ denotes the oxidising half of the salt redox couple. In practice
the fluorine potential is set by a buffer, either beryllium metal or an
imposed $\mathrm{HF/H_2}$ ratio in the cover gas, so we write channel F with an
effective activity $a_\mathrm{F}$ lumping the fluoride activity and the redox
potential,

\begin{equation}
    w_{\mathrm{F}} = k_{\mathrm{f}}^{+}\, a_{\mathrm{F}}\,
                     \cm{\mathrm{H}}\big|_\Gamma
                   - k_{\mathrm{f}}^{-}\,\cs{\mathrm{HF}}\big|_\Gamma ,
    \label{eq:model3_rate}
\end{equation}

while $w_\mathrm{rec}$ retains the form of Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}). The
atomic flux leaving the metal is now shared between the two,

\begin{equation}
    -D^{\mathrm{m}}\nabla \cm{\mathrm{H}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\big|_\Gamma
    = 2\,w_{\mathrm{rec}} + w_{\mathrm{F}} ,
    \label{eq:model3_total_flux}
\end{equation}

and each channel feeds its own carrier in the salt through
Eq.~(\ref{eq:bc_salt}).

The two channels are of different order in the interfacial loading: R is
quadratic in $\cm{\mathrm{H}}$ and F is linear. Their competition is measured
by the branching ratio

\begin{equation}
    \Bra
    \equiv \frac{w_{\mathrm{F}}}{2\,w_{\mathrm{rec}}}
    \;\xrightarrow[\text{far from equilibrium}]{}\;
    \frac{k_{\mathrm{f}}^{+} a_{\mathrm{F}}}
         {2\,k_{\mathrm{r}}^{+}\,\cm{\mathrm{H}}\big|_\Gamma} ,
    \label{eq:branching}
\end{equation}

whose consequences form the core of the argument of this paper.

First, $\Bra$ depends \emph{inversely} on the interfacial loading. At low
loading the fluorination channel dominates and the atomic flux crossing the
interface scales linearly with $\cm{\mathrm{H}}$; at high loading recombination
takes over and the scaling becomes quadratic. Defining the apparent interfacial
exponent on that flux, the right-hand side of Eq.~(\ref{eq:bc_metal}) for
$i = \mathrm{H}$,

\begin{equation}
    n \equiv \frac{\partial \ln J}{\partial \ln \cm{\mathrm{H}}} ,
    \qquad J \equiv \sum_r \nu_{\mathrm{H}r}\, w_r ,
    \qquad 1 \le n \le 2 ,
    \label{eq:apparent_exponent}
\end{equation}

LTE requires $n$ to be a constant fixed in advance: $n = 2$ for the
Sieverts/Henry closure of Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}), $n = 1$ for a Henry/Henry-like
closure. Here $n$ is instead a solution-dependent quantity that drifts during a
transient as the interfacial loading builds up. No fixed algebraic interface
law can reproduce this behaviour.

The same slope can be read off the salt-side inventory instead of the flux, as a
fitted solubility does, and the two are not the same number unless the carriers
are transported alike (App.~\ref{app:analytical},
Sec.~\ref{sec:exponent_verification}). We take the flux as the definition
throughout, since it is what a permeation experiment measures directly.

Second, $\Bra$ depends on the salt redox state through $a_\mathrm{F}$, which is
itself dynamic: HF production, corrosion of the container and depletion of the
buffer all move it. The behaviour of the interface is therefore coupled to the
chemistry of the salt, and is not a material property of the metal/salt pair
that could be tabulated once and reused.

Third, fast kinetics does not rescue LTE here. Even when
both channels are individually equilibrated, with a large Damk\"ohler number
for each in the sense of Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler}, the \emph{partition} of the
flux between them is set by the ratio of the forward rate constants and not by
thermodynamics. Making each channel faster makes each channel individually
closer to its own equilibrium while leaving the split between them kinetic.

One objection to all of this is worth answering in full. It is possible in
principle to close the interface without any kinetic information, by imposing
full local chemical equilibrium among all interfacial species, a Gibbs
minimisation at $\Gamma$ at the prescribed fluorine potential. That closure is, however, (i) not what any macroscopic
hydrogen transport code implements; (ii) valid only if every interconversion is
fast compared with transport, which is exactly what the available evidence puts
in doubt \cite{hodille_molecular_2022,calderoni_measurement_2008}; and
(iii) still silent on how a given net flux divides between carriers, since
equilibrium fixes concentrations and not fluxes. The last point is the decisive
one for the quantity measured in a permeation experiment.

\subsection{Multiple isotopes: isotopologue channels}
\label{sec:isotopes}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The framework extends to $N_\mathrm{iso}$ mobile atomic species in the metal
without modification, but the structure that emerges is qualitatively new.
Recombination now populates every isotopologue and fluorination every fluoride.
The gas/solid case has already met this structure: co-permeation of hydrogen
isotopes through a metal forms the mixed molecule at the surface through a
bilinear rate \cite{zhu_modeling_2024}, and the solubility of hydrogen from
mixed gases is treated through the exchange equilibrium between isotopologues,
and not one species at a time \cite{san_marchi_solubility_2008}, whose rates
have been measured directly in H/D/T mixtures \cite{ida_hydrogen_1992}.
For $\{\mathrm{H,T}\}$ the recombination channels are

\begin{align}
    w_{\mathrm{HH}} &= k_{\mathrm{HH}}^{+}\left(\cm{\mathrm{H}}\right)^{2}
                     - k_{\mathrm{HH}}^{-}\,\cs{\mathrm{H_2}}
    \label{eq:iso_HH}\\
    w_{\mathrm{HT}} &= k_{\mathrm{HT}}^{+}\,\cm{\mathrm{H}}\,\cm{\mathrm{T}}
                     - k_{\mathrm{HT}}^{-}\,\cs{\mathrm{HT}}
    \label{eq:iso_HT}\\
    w_{\mathrm{TT}} &= k_{\mathrm{TT}}^{+}\left(\cm{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{2}
                     - k_{\mathrm{TT}}^{-}\,\cs{\mathrm{T_2}}
    \label{eq:iso_TT}
\end{align}

and the fluorination channels are

\begin{align}
    w_{\mathrm{HF}} &= k_{\mathrm{f,H}}^{+} a_{\mathrm{F}} \cm{\mathrm{H}}
                     - k_{\mathrm{f,H}}^{-}\,\cs{\mathrm{HF}}
    \label{eq:iso_HF}\\
    w_{\mathrm{TF}} &= k_{\mathrm{f,T}}^{+} a_{\mathrm{F}} \cm{\mathrm{T}}
                     - k_{\mathrm{f,T}}^{-}\,\cs{\mathrm{TF}} .
    \label{eq:iso_TF}
\end{align}

The metal-side conditions follow from Eq.~(\ref{eq:bc_metal}) with the
stoichiometry of Eq.~(\ref{eq:atom_balance}),

\begin{align}
    -D^{\mathrm{m}}_\mathrm{H}\nabla \cm{\mathrm{H}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\big|_\Gamma
        &= 2\,w_{\mathrm{HH}} + w_{\mathrm{HT}} + w_{\mathrm{HF}}
    \label{eq:iso_flux_H}\\
    -D^{\mathrm{m}}_\mathrm{T}\nabla \cm{\mathrm{T}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\big|_\Gamma
        &= 2\,w_{\mathrm{TT}} + w_{\mathrm{HT}} + w_{\mathrm{TF}} ,
    \label{eq:iso_flux_T}
\end{align}

each salt-side carrier being fed by the single channel that produces it.

All five channels are written with the plain mass-action form of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:mass_action}), with no combinatorial prefactor, as \FESTIM{} does
throughout for volumetric reactions, gas/solid surface reactions and interfacial
channels alike. Stoichiometry enters only through the coefficients $\nu_{ir}$ of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:bc_metal}).

The statistical degeneracy of the mixed pair is therefore a statement about the
\emph{value} of $k_{\mathrm{HT}}^{+}$, not an extra factor in the rate law.
Counting encounters fixes it. The number of distinct identical pairs available
per unit area is $\tfrac{1}{2}(\cm{\mathrm{H}})^2$, whereas an H and a T are
distinguishable and give $\cm{\mathrm{H}}\cm{\mathrm{T}}$ distinct pairs, so
writing the homonuclear channels as $k_{\mathrm{HH}}^{+}(\cm{\mathrm{H}})^2$
absorbs the one-half and the same per-encounter constant corresponds to

\begin{equation}
    k_{\mathrm{HT}}^{+} = 2\,k_{\mathrm{HH}}^{+} = 2\,k_{\mathrm{TT}}^{+}
    \qquad \text{(mass-independent limit)} ,
    \label{eq:stat_factor}
\end{equation}

with the reverse constants equal. Equilibrating
Eqs.~(\ref{eq:iso_HH})--(\ref{eq:iso_TT}) under Eq.~(\ref{eq:stat_factor}) then
gives

\begin{equation}
    K_\mathrm{exch}
    = \frac{\left(\cs{\mathrm{HT}}\right)^{2}}{\cs{\mathrm{H_2}}\,\cs{\mathrm{T_2}}}
    = 4 ,
    \label{eq:kexch}
\end{equation}

the classical value for
$\mathrm{H_2} + \mathrm{T_2} \rightleftharpoons 2\,\mathrm{HT}$ that follows
from the symmetry numbers $\sigma_{\mathrm{H_2}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{T_2}} = 2$
and $\sigma_{\mathrm{HT}} = 1$, and that states that random pairing of equal H
and T populations yields $\mathrm{H_2 : HT : T_2} = 1 : 2 : 1$. Overlooking
Eq.~(\ref{eq:stat_factor}) and taking all three forward constants equal instead
yields $K_\mathrm{exch} = 1$, an equal split, and no scrambling;
App.~\ref{app:conservation} reproduces both cases numerically. Any departure
from $K_\mathrm{exch} = 4$ is a genuine isotope effect and belongs in the rate
constants themselves, the zero-point-energy corrections being collected in
App.~\ref{app:balance}.

The counting makes the multi-isotope case structurally different. Two mobile
species on the metal side feed five carriers on the salt side,

\begin{equation}
    N_\mathrm{iso} \;\longrightarrow\;
    \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}N_\mathrm{iso}(N_\mathrm{iso}+1)}_{\text{molecular}}
    + \underbrace{N_\mathrm{iso}}_{\text{fluoride}},
    \qquad
    \mathrm{(H,T)}:\; 2 \rightarrow 3 + 2 = 5 ,
    \label{eq:counting}
\end{equation}

whereas LTE supplies one scalar constraint per transported species pair. With
two metal-side and five salt-side interfacial unknowns the per-species
Dirichlet closure is underdetermined. The more serious objection is that it is
also wrong in form: by Eq.~(\ref{eq:iso_HT}) the HT concentration is set by
the \emph{product} $\cm{\mathrm{H}}\cm{\mathrm{T}}$, so the
metal~$\rightarrow$~salt map is bilinear and non-diagonal, and a condition of
the form $c_i/K_{S,i} = f(c_i')$ cannot represent an off-diagonal coupling. In
the multi-isotope case LTE is not inaccurate; it is ill-posed.

Isotope exchange, $\mathrm{H_2} + \mathrm{T_2} \rightleftharpoons
2\,\mathrm{HT}$, is slow in the homogeneous phase but is catalysed by metal
surfaces. In the present framework it requires no additional reaction: it
emerges from Eqs.~(\ref{eq:iso_HH})--(\ref{eq:iso_TT}), since a $\mathrm{T_2}$
molecule may dissociate at $\Gamma$ and deposit T into the lattice, and lattice
T may subsequently leave paired with an H. Interfacial scrambling is thus
predicted by the model, not imposed on it.

Two observable consequences follow, neither of which is expressible under LTE.
The first is \emph{isotope swamping}: the tritium flux depends on the protium
inventory, because raising $\cm{\mathrm{H}}$ shifts tritium from the
$\mathrm{T_2}$ channel to the statistically favoured HT channel and thereby
changes the total tritium throughput. A diagonal closure predicts no such
coupling. The second is \emph{interfacial fractionation}: because the
recombination and fluorination channels carry different isotope dependences
($k_\mathrm{HH}$ against $k_\mathrm{HT}$ against $k_\mathrm{TT}$;
$k_\mathrm{f,H}$ against $k_\mathrm{f,T}$), the isotopic composition of the
permeating flux differs from that of the metal, and differs between the
molecular and the fluoride carrier. We report it as the model output
$\alpha = (\mathrm{T/H})_\mathrm{flux} / (\mathrm{T/H})_\mathrm{metal}$.

Detailed balance applies channel by channel as in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_general}), the ratios $k^{+}/k^{-}$ being
constrained by the isotopologue equilibrium constants. These are close to, but
not exactly, the classical statistical values, the departure arising from
zero-point energy; they are collected in App.~\ref{app:balance}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Dimensionless criteria for LTE validity}
\label{sec:damkohler}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The framework of Sec.~\ref{sec:general} contains LTE as a limit, so it can also
say when that limit is legitimate. Three questions have to be answered, in
increasing order of structural severity.

\paragraph{Within a channel} The relevant comparison is between interfacial
equilibration and bulk transport. A dimensionless group of this kind is the
standard way of deciding whether a local equilibrium assumption is admissible,
both for transport-coupled interfacial chemistry \cite{knapp_spatial_1989} and,
under the name of a surface-limited regime, for gas-driven permeation through
metals \cite{pisarev_gas-driven_2003,denisov_surface-limited_2018}. Because
channels may be of different order,
the comparison is made with the exchange velocity obtained by linearising the
channel about the interfacial state,
$k_r^\mathrm{eff} \equiv \partial w_r / \partial \cm{}\big|_\Gamma$, which
reduces to $k^{+}$ for the first-order Model 1 and to
$2 k_\mathrm{r}^{+} \cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma$ for the recombination channel of
Model 2. The Damk\"ohler number of channel $r$ is then

\begin{equation}
    \Da_r = \frac{k_r^\mathrm{eff}\,L}{D} .
    \label{eq:damkohler}
\end{equation}

When $\Da_r \gg 1$ the channel is locally equilibrated and its algebraic LTE
form is recovered, as shown in Secs.~\ref{sec:model1} and~\ref{sec:model2}. For
asymmetric systems one Damk\"ohler number should be evaluated per side, using
$D_A, L_A$ and $D_B, L_B$ respectively, and the smaller of the two governs.

For a channel of order one, as in Model 1, $k^\mathrm{eff}$ is a constant and
$\Da$ is an input to the problem. For the recombination channel of Model 2 it is
neither: the metal side sees an exchange velocity
$2k_\mathrm{r}^{+}\cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma$, which falls as the interface is
depleted, so the same interface may be effectively equilibrated at high loading
while being kinetically limited early in a permeation transient, before the
upstream side has charged. Two consequences follow. The additive interfacial
resistance of App.~\ref{app:analytical} is exact only for Model 1, holding for
Model 2 about an operating point alone. And a parametric sweep in $\Da$ requires
a reference loading to be fixed: we use the upstream Sieverts value
$c^{\star} = K_S\sqrt{P_\mathrm{up}}$, giving the control parameter
$2k_\mathrm{r}^{+}c^{\star}L/D$, and report the local value attained at the
interface as a diagnostic.

The caveat carried over from Ref.~\cite{silva-solis_hydrogen_2026} applies
throughout: $\Da \gg 1$ is \emph{necessary but not sufficient}, since under a
net flux the steady state departs from equilibrium by a term proportional to
$\phi/k^{+}$, which LTE sets to zero by construction.

A second and independent separation of timescales is implied whenever the
interface is treated as having no state of its own.
Equation~(\ref{eq:damkohler}) compares an interfacial rate with bulk transport
and says nothing about how long an interfacial population, where there is one,
takes to relax; writing the interface condition algebraically in the trace
values requires that relaxation to be fast compared with $L^{2}/D$, as set out
in App.~\ref{app:limits}. For W/Cu the separation is wide, three to six orders
of magnitude between interfacial equilibration and permeation
\cite{silva-solis_hydrogen_2026}. Where no such comparison is available, the
algebraic form is an assumption in its own right and should be reported as one.

\paragraph{Between channels} The relevant group is the branching ratio $\Bra$
of Eq.~(\ref{eq:branching}). In the limits $\Bra \rightarrow 0$ and
$\Bra \rightarrow \infty$ one channel dominates and an LTE form exists,
Sieverts/Henry and linear respectively. When $\Bra = \mathcal{O}(1)$, no
single algebraic law applies at all, however large the Damk\"ohler numbers are.

The two groups are independent, and Fig.~\ref{fig:regime_map} puts them on the
two axes of a single map. The field plotted is the larger of two quantities,

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E} = \max\left(
        \underbrace{\frac{1}{1 + \Da}}_{\text{within a channel}} ,\;
        \underbrace{\frac{\min(1, \Bra)}{1 + \Bra}}_{\text{between channels}}
    \right) ,
    \label{eq:lte_indicator}
\end{equation}

the first being the relative error made on the flux by treating a channel as
equilibrated, exact for Model 1 by Eq.~(\ref{eq:model1_convergence}), and the
second the fraction of the atomic flux carried by the minority channel, the part
of the flux that no single-channel closure represents at all.
Taking the larger of the two is a convention: $\mathcal{E}$ is an indicator of
how badly the best available LTE closure does, not a bound on the error of any
particular simulation. The map is nevertheless entirely analytical, and the
right-hand axis carries the apparent exponent
$n = (2+\Bra)/(1+\Bra)$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:n_of_B}), the quantity a permeation
experiment would report.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{regime_map.pdf}
    \caption{Where a local-equilibrium interface is defensible. The indicator
    $\mathcal{E}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_indicator}) over the $(\Da, \Bra)$ plane,
    with contours at \qty{1}{\percent} and \qty{10}{\percent}. The abscissa is
    the two-sided group
    $\Da^{\star}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:defect_1_explicit}), the ratio of the total
    bulk resistance to the interfacial resistance $1/k^{+}$, since that is the
    combination the flux error depends on.
    LTE requires both a fast channel and a
    dominant one: the pale regions at bottom right and top right are the
    Sieverts/Henry and linear closures, the left-hand strip is kinetically
    limited whatever the branching, and the band around $\Bra = 1$ admits no
    algebraic interface law however fast the kinetics. The right-hand axis gives
    the apparent exponent $n$ corresponding to each branching ratio.}
    \label{fig:regime_map}
\end{figure}

Read as a decision aid, the map says that an LTE interface needs $\Da \gtrsim
10$ \emph{and} a branching ratio at least an order of magnitude away from unity.
Neither condition alone suffices, and the second is the one that the literature
does not check.

\paragraph{Across isotopes} LTE further requires the metal~$\rightarrow$~salt
map to be diagonal. The departure is quantified by the fraction of the tritium
flux carried by the mixed isotopologue,

\begin{equation}
    \chi_\mathrm{HT} = \frac{w_\mathrm{HT}}{w_\mathrm{HT} + 2 w_\mathrm{TT}} .
    \label{eq:chi_HT}
\end{equation}

A diagonal, per-isotope description is recovered as
$\chi_\mathrm{HT} \rightarrow 0$ and is forbidden when
$\chi_\mathrm{HT} = \mathcal{O}(1)$. In the tritium-lean, protium-rich
conditions typical of a breeding blanket, $\chi_\mathrm{HT} \rightarrow 1$.

\section{Implementation in \FESTIM}
\label{sec:implementation}
% ===============================================================

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Weak formulation}
\label{sec:weak}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Each subdomain is discretised on its own submesh of a common parent mesh and
carries its own function space for every species it hosts, so that the
concentration fields are free to jump across $\Gamma$. This is the multi-species
discontinuous architecture of \FESTIM{}~v2 \cite{dark_festim_2026}, built on
submeshes and entity maps in DOLFINx \cite{baratta_dolfinx_2023}. Multiplying
the transport equation of species $i$ on $\Omega_A$ by a test function
$v \in H^1(\Omega_A)$ and integrating the diffusive term by parts gives the
usual bulk residual, and the interface enters only through the part of the
boundary integral carried by $\Gamma$. Substituting Eqs.~(\ref{eq:bc_metal})
and~(\ref{eq:bc_salt}) leaves the surface contributions

\begin{align}
    F^{\mathrm{m}}_i &\supset
    \phantom{-}\int_\Gamma \Big(\sum_r \nu_{ir}\, w_r\Big)\,
        v^{\mathrm{m}}_i \,\mathrm{d}S
    \label{eq:weak_metal}\\
    F^{\mathrm{s}}_\alpha &\supset
    -\int_\Gamma \Big(\sum_r \mu_{\alpha r}\, w_r\Big)\,
        v^{\mathrm{s}}_\alpha \,\mathrm{d}S ,
    \label{eq:weak_salt}
\end{align}

with $w_r$ given by Eq.~(\ref{eq:mass_action}) and evaluated on the traces of
the solution from either side. The opposite signs are those of the two outward
normals: a channel proceeding forwards removes atoms from $\Omega_A$ and
delivers carriers to $\Omega_B$. Because $\Gamma$ is an interior facet of the
parent mesh, the two integrals are assembled with an interior-facet measure
whose integration data map each facet to the cell it belongs to on each side,
one restriction per subdomain.

\KEY{No interfacial degrees of freedom are introduced.} Every channel is
algebraic in the trace values, so no function space on $\Gamma$ and no
additional unknowns are required, and the size of the linear system is unchanged
relative to an LTE interface on the same mesh. This is the simplification
relative to the companion codimension-1 work \cite{festim_codim1_inprep}, where
an interfacial inventory is transported along $\Gamma$ and does carry its own
degrees of freedom.

The stoichiometric coefficients enter as multiplicities: the rate $w_r$ is added
once to the residual of each occurrence of a species in the channel, so
declaring the recombination channel of Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}) with two H
reactants produces both the square in the rate law and the factor two in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:bc_metal}). The statistical degeneracy of the mixed isotopologue,
Eq.~(\ref{eq:stat_factor}), cannot be encoded the same way, and the natural
guess is wrong: $w_r$ carries both directions, so declaring the HT channel twice
with the reactants in either order doubles the forward and the reverse term
equally, rescaling the channel in time while leaving $K_\mathrm{exch}$ at unity.
The degeneracy is a forward-only count and belongs in the value of
$k^{+}_\mathrm{HT}$.

The assembled system is blocked, with one residual block per subdomain, and the
surface integrals of Eqs.~(\ref{eq:weak_metal}) and~(\ref{eq:weak_salt}) are the
only terms coupling one block to another. Each block is differentiated
symbolically with respect to every unknown of the coupled system, so the
Jacobian is exact by construction and its sparsity pattern follows the channels
declared. The isotopologue channels populate a block that no per-species closure
possesses: since $\partial w_\mathrm{HT}/\partial \cm{\mathrm{H}} =
k^{+}_\mathrm{HT}\cm{\mathrm{T}}$, the residual of H acquires a block against the
trial function of T, so two species on the \emph{same} side of the interface are
coupled to each other through the interface. Several channels sharing one
interface are declared as separate objects whose contributions add, and this is
how we assemble Model 3 and the five-channel isotopologue set of
Sec.~\ref{sec:isotopes}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Parameterisation and numerical behaviour}
\label{sec:params}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Each channel needs two numbers, and they are of different kinds. Detailed
balance, Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_general}), fixes the \emph{ratio}
$k_r^{+}/k_r^{-}$ from the same thermodynamic data that parameterise the LTE
conditions of Sec.~\ref{sec:lte}, so no new information is needed there. The
\emph{magnitude} is the additional input the framework requires, and it can come
from atomistics, from fitting to permeation breakthrough transients, or, as a
first estimate for the recombination channel, from the gas/metal recombination
coefficients measured for the same metal. Silva-Sol\'is et al.\
\cite{silva-solis_hydrogen_2026} follow this division of labour for W/Cu, fixing
the ratios from the solution energies of the interfacial and bulk sites
\cite{silva-solis_solution_2024} and fitting only the magnitude to the
relaxation time of a site network computed from density functional theory. The
sensitivity of a prediction to that magnitude is exactly what the Damk\"ohler
number of Eq.~(\ref{eq:damkohler}) measures: where $\Da \gg 1$ an order of
magnitude in $k^{+}$ is immaterial, and where it is not, the interface is
rate-limiting and the number has to be justified.

Two properties of the present implementation are limitations of the code and not
of the framework. The rate constants are prescribed as plain numbers, with no
Arrhenius form, so a temperature dependence has to be imposed by the user and
the activity $a_\mathrm{F}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:model3_rate}) is folded into
$k_\mathrm{f}^{+}$ by hand. More importantly, the ratio $k_r^{+}/k_r^{-}$ is not
checked against Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_general}) by the solver:
thermodynamic consistency is a property of how the model is parameterised, and
it remains the responsibility of whoever writes the input.

\section{Verification}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Recovery of LTE in the fast-kinetics limit}
\label{sec:lte_limit}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

We check that each model recovers its LTE condition at large rate constants, and
that it does so at the rate predicted in App.~\ref{app:limits}. Both tests are
run against the closed-form steady states of App.~\ref{app:analytical}, which
are finite algebraic expressions with no series truncation, so the residual
differences quoted below are those of the numerical solution alone.

The first test is Model 1 on a one-dimensional two-slab problem, with
$D_A = 0.5$ and $D_B = 1$ in arbitrary units, slabs of equal thickness $0.5$,
concentrations $c_0 = 2$ and $c_L = 1$ imposed at the outer faces, and
$k^{-}/k^{+} = 0.5$. The steady problem is solved for seventeen values of the
Damk\"ohler number spanning eight decades, $\Da = k^{+}L_A/D_A$ from $10^{-2}$
to $10^{6}$. Figure~\ref{fig:two_slab} shows the problem and the reading of its
steady solution that the rest of this section uses: plotting the two slabs on a
common scale, $c_A$ on one side and $c_B/K$ on the other, turns
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss}) into continuity, so what is left at $\Gamma$ is the defect
$\Delta = \phi/k^{+}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:defect_1_explicit}). It closes as $\Da$
grows and is nonzero at any finite rate constant, because the interface is
carrying a flux. The transient behaviour over the same sweep,
Fig.~\ref{fig:lte_transient}, makes the first of the three consequences listed
in Sec.~\ref{sec:lte} concrete: at moderate $\Da$ the interfacial ratio is far
from its equilibrium value for most of the transient, the timescale an algebraic
closure denies the interface.

The second test replaces the channel with the recombination channel of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}) on a metal/liquid pair, the case this paper is about.
Geometry, diffusivities and outer concentrations are those of the Model 1 test,
so that only the interface changes: the slab $[0, 0.5]$ is the metal and carries
the atomic species $\mathrm{H}$, the slab $[0.5, 1]$ is the liquid and carries
the molecular carrier $\mathrm{H_2}$, and the two are now distinct species and
not one species restricted to two subdomains. The channel is declared with two
reactants and one product, so that the stoichiometric factor two of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:bc_metal}) follows from the declaration and is not imposed by hand,
and detailed balance is applied through Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_2}) with
$K_H/K_S^{2} = 0.5$. Since the channel is second order, $\Da$ is not an input to
the problem: we follow Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler} and sweep the control parameter
$2k_\mathrm{r}^{+}c_0L_\mathrm{m}/D_\mathrm{m}$ over the same eight decades,
reporting the value attained at the interface as a diagnostic.

Damk\"ohler number attained at the interface settles at $3/4$ of the control

arbitrarily far below the control one in a thick metal. The second feature is
that the additive decomposition of Fig.~\ref{fig:two_slab}b does not survive:
$1/k_\mathrm{r}^{+}$ is not a resistance in series with the bulk ones, and it is
only about an operating point that
$(2k_\mathrm{r}^{+}\cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma)^{-1}$ plays that role.

\subsection{The apparent exponent of competing channels}
\label{sec:exponent_verification}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The two tests above each equilibrate a single channel. The third exercises the
configuration for which no algebraic closure exists at all, two channels
competing on the same interface, and checks the exponent they produce against
Eq.~(\ref{eq:n_of_B}). Geometry and metal-side transport are again those of
Sec.~\ref{sec:lte_limit}. The liquid now carries two species, $\mathrm{H_2}$ fed
by the recombination channel of Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}) and HF fed by the
fluorination channel of Eq.~(\ref{eq:model3_rate}), declared as two interface
objects sharing one interface identifier so that their residual contributions
add as described in Sec.~\ref{sec:weak}. Both carriers are held at zero on the
outer liquid face, the swept-salt regime in which Eq.~(\ref{eq:n_of_B}) was
derived, and they are given deliberately different diffusivities,
$D_\mathrm{HF} = D_\mathrm{H_2}/4$, for a reason that appears below. The
upstream loading is swept over five decades at three values of $a_\mathrm{F}$,
183 steady solves in all, which carries the branching ratio of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:branching}) from \num{2.1e-3} to \num{6.0e3}.

Nothing about the exponent is imposed on the solver, and this is what makes the
test meaningful. We measure it afterwards, as the logarithmic slope of the total
atomic flux against the interfacial loading taken by centred differences along
the sweep, the same operation a permeation experiment performs on its own data.
The computed interfacial concentrations agree with the closed form of
App.~\ref{app:analytical} to a median relative error of \num{2.2e-16} over the
whole set, and at worst \num{1.8e-8} at the two smallest loadings of the most
oxidising sweep, where the interfacial concentration is itself \num{4e-4} and
the solver tolerance sets the floor.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{verification_model3_exponent.pdf}
    \caption{Two channels on one interface, and the exponent they produce.
    (a) The apparent exponent $n$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:apparent_exponent}) against
    the interfacial loading, for three values of the effective fluoride activity
    $a_\mathrm{F}$: lines are Eq.~(\ref{eq:n_of_B}), circles the slopes measured
    from the computed fluxes. Every sweep leaves the linear closure at low
    loading and approaches Sieverts/Henry at high loading, and the redox state
    sets where the crossover falls, not whether it happens.
    (b) The two ways of reading the exponent, against the branching ratio
    itself. Both follow Eq.~(\ref{eq:n_of_B}), the flux in $\Bra$ and the
    salt-side inventory in $\Bra\,R_\mathrm{HF}/R_\mathrm{H_2}$, so the curves
    have the same shape a constant factor apart: they agree on the law and not
    on the number. The arrow marks the separation of their $n = 3/2$ crossings,
    the resistance ratio, here $4$.}
    \label{fig:model3_exponent}
\end{figure}

The measured exponent tracks Eq.~(\ref{eq:n_of_B}) to within \num{5.8e-4}
everywhere, the truncation error of the centred difference at the sweep spacing
used, and it spans $1.0002$ to $1.9978$ as the branching ratio crosses the unit
interval. At the point of the sweep nearest $\Bra = 1$ we obtain $n = 1.5000$,
the midpoint of Eq.~(\ref{eq:n_of_B}) at which the two channels carry equal
atomic fluxes. Read together with Fig.~\ref{fig:model3_exponent}a, this is the
falsifiable content of Sec.~\ref{sec:units} in verified form: the exponent is a
continuous function of the branching ratio over its whole range, so a redox
sweep moves it and no fixed choice of $1$ or $2$ describes the interface across
such a sweep. The reverse terms of both channels are active throughout and leave
the exponent untouched, because eliminating the salt-side traces renormalises
each forward constant by a factor independent of the loading
(App.~\ref{app:analytical}), so Eq.~(\ref{eq:n_of_B}) holds beyond the
irreversible regime in which it was derived.

The inventory variant of Eq.~(\ref{eq:apparent_exponent}) is a different number,
and this is why we gave the two carriers different diffusivities. The
interfacial inventory $2\cs{\mathrm{H_2}} + \cs{\mathrm{HF}}$ weights each
channel by the downstream resistance its carrier meets, so its logarithmic slope
follows Eq.~(\ref{eq:n_of_B}) with $\Bra$ replaced by
$\Bra\,R_\mathrm{HF}/R_\mathrm{H_2}$, and the sweep reproduces this to
\num{5.7e-4}. Figure~\ref{fig:model3_exponent}b shows the two readouts on the
same abscissa, where they are curves of identical shape separated by the
resistance ratio: the flux exponent passes through $3/2$ at $\Bra = 1$ and the
inventory exponent does so at $\Bra = 1/4$. At a branching ratio of $0.31$, for
instance, the flux reports $n = 1.77$ and the inventory $n = 1.45$ on the same
interface at the same instant. An exponent fitted to a sampled salt inventory
and one fitted to a measured permeation flux therefore imply different branching
ratios unless the two carriers are transported alike, and reading the first as
though it were the second misplaces $\Bra$ by the resistance ratio.

% ===============================================================
\section{Application to a representative Ni/FLiBe system}
\label{sec:hyperion}
% ===============================================================

The three tests of Sec.~\ref{sec:verification} are dimensionless. In this
section we place the framework at a dimensional operating point taken from an
experiment, so that the groups the earlier sections treat as parameters can be
read as rate constants at a temperature and a geometry somebody is running.

The scope is deliberately narrow. Nothing below is fitted and nothing is
compared with a measurement. \HYPERION{} supplies a realistic geometry, an
operating window and a set of literature transport properties, and the
simulations report what the kinetic framework predicts there. Every result is
read against the LTE closure applied to the same problem, and not against data.
Inferring interfacial rate constants from measured transients belongs to the
experimental campaign; Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations} states what that would
require.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{System description and model setup}
\label{sec:hyperion_setup}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

\HYPERION{} (HYdrogen PERmeatION) at the MIT Plasma Science and Fusion Center
\cite{hyperion_facility} drives hydrogen isotopes from a gas plenum, through a
Ni membrane, into a molten FLiBe pool held in a Ni container. The free surface of
the salt is swept by a cover gas and the released flux is measured. The pumped
fluoride salt loop of Ref.~\cite{fuerst_molten_2025} addresses the same
speciation question in a flowing configuration.
\TODO{state the sweep gas and the diagnostics (QMS, bubblers, ionisation

Three interfaces appear in the problem. The gas/Ni interface is closed by the
existing dissociation-recombination boundary condition and the FLiBe/cover gas
interface releases the carriers from the free surface. The Ni/FLiBe interface is
the one of interest, and it is the configuration of Sec.~\ref{sec:model3}, with
a recombination channel and a fluorination channel competing on the same
surface. Calderoni et al.\ \cite{calderoni_measurement_2008} measured tritium
permeation through Ni into FLiBe and could not determine whether the tritium
travelled as a fluoride-bound species, as HT, or as a mixture of the two. Under
LTE that question has to be answered before the simulation is set up, since the
carrier is what the solubility law refers to. In the kinetic framework it is a
branching ratio, and so a quantity the model reports.

Figure~\ref{fig:hyperion_geometry} shows the vessel and the reduction we apply
to it. The vessel is an axisymmetric nickel can of inner radius
\qty{39.0}{\milli\metre} and outer radius \qty{41.0}{\milli\metre}; a membrane
separates a \qty{20}{\milli\metre} upstream plenum from a FLiBe pool
\qty{5.14}{\milli\metre} deep at \qty{500}{\degreeCelsius}, with a cover-gas
space above. The one-dimensional model keeps the membrane and the pool and drops
the rest. What that discards can be sized: the salt wets
\qty{1.26e-3}{\square\metre} of sidewall against \qty{4.78e-3}{\square\metre} of
membrane, so the omitted nickel carries a permeation path in parallel with the
modelled one worth \qty{26}{\percent} of the membrane area. The multidimensional
treatment of that geometry is the subject of Ref.~\cite{hyperion_multidim}. We
use the one-dimensional reduction to demonstrate the interface models and not to
infer properties from data, so the omitted path is a caveat and not an error bar.

Three derived quantities set the run length and the centres of the sweeps that
follow. The two bulk diffusion times are $L^{2}/D = \qty{2.75e3}{\second}$ in
the metal and \qty{1.95e4}{\second} in the salt, so the salt is the slow leg by
a factor of seven and the interfacial resistance competes against a well-defined
transport time. The Sieverts loading on the upstream face is
\qty{1.03e25}{\per\cubic\metre}, of which the LTE steady state leaves
\qty{63}{\percent} at the interface, so neither leg is negligible. The LTE steady
flux is \qty{2.81e18}{\per\square\metre\per\second}, or
\qty{1.34e16}{\per\second} over the membrane. Every transient reported below runs
to \qty{e5}{\second}, about five salt diffusion times.

The fluorination channel has no measured thermodynamics to anchor to. No Henry
constant for HF in FLiBe appears in the compilations we use, and by the argument
of Sec.~\ref{sec:units} there could not be one that is independent of an assumed
carrier. What defines the channel is the pair
$(k_\mathrm{f}^{+}a_\mathrm{F},\, k_\mathrm{f}^{-})$, and detailed balance fixes
only their ratio, so we do not give the channel a solubility at all. It is
parameterised by the two dimensionless groups the paper has already defined, and
the results of Sec.~\ref{sec:hyperion_branching} are reported against $\Bra$.
That the community has reported $K_H$ for the molecular carrier in FLiBe from
several studies and none for the fluoride carrier is itself a consequence of
assumption (H3): the carrier was chosen before the measurement was reduced.

The baseline against which everything below is read closes the interface the way
a macroscopic code closes it, with Sieverts on the nickel against Henry in the
salt, Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}), imposed as the algebraic constraint of
Sec.~\ref{sec:lte} through the penalty formulation \FESTIM{} already provides.
At the final time the computed interfacial concentrations and the downstream flux
match the closed form of the two-slab LTE problem to \num{1.1e-6} and
\num{3.4e-6} respectively, and the interface constraint itself holds to
\num{3.6e-7}, so the baseline is a reference curve and not a second numerical
solution carrying an error budget of its own. Reaching that agreement requires a
nonlinear tolerance set from the residual of the interface term and not from the
magnitude of the unknowns: with $c \sim \qty{e25}{\per\cubic\metre}$ an absolute
tolerance of \num{e15} stops the Newton iteration early and leaves the final
state a few parts per thousand from the closed form, an error that grows under
mesh refinement and is easily misread as a discretisation error.

\subsection{Single isotope, single channel: LTE recovered}
\label{sec:hyperion_lte}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

We first switch the fluorination channel off, which corresponds to a strongly
reducing salt, and let hydrogen cross the interface by recombination alone. The
channel is that of Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}), and its reverse constant is fixed
by Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_2}) from the same $K_S$ and $K_H$ that
parameterise the baseline. The forward constant is then the only number left
free, and we report it as the Damk\"ohler number
$\Da = k_\mathrm{r}^{+}\,c^{\star}L_\mathrm{Ni}/D_\mathrm{Ni}$ with $c^{\star}$
the LTE interfacial loading, which makes $\Da$ an input to a sweep instead of an
output of one.
\VERIFY{this section builds $\Da$ from $k_\mathrm{r}^{+}c^{\star}$ while
Eq.~(\ref{eq:damkohler}) linearises the channel to
$2k_\mathrm{r}^{+}\cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma$, so the two differ by a factor of
two. Reconcile the two conventions, in the scripts as well as in the text,
before submission.}

Sweeping $\Da$ over six decades at fixed thermodynamics, geometry and transport,
the computed steady state agrees with the closed form of the single-channel
problem to a relative \num{1.4e-5} at the slow end of the sweep and to better
than \num{6e-6} everywhere else. The steady flux approaches the LTE one from
below and first order in $1/\Da$: taking $J/J_\mathrm{LTE}$, we obtain $0.0782$
at $\Da = 10^{-2}$, $0.883$ at $\Da = 1$, $0.987$ at $\Da = 10$, $0.9987$ at
$\Da = 10^{2}$ and $0.99998$ at $\Da = 10^{4}$. That the two closed forms are
nested is not a numerical result: the coefficient of the Model 2 quadratic tends
term by term to the coefficient of the LTE quadratic as $k_\mathrm{r}^{+}$ grows
at fixed ratio, so the agreement above tests the implementation and not the
definition.

Figure~\ref{fig:hyperion_damkohler} shows the transients themselves. The steady
flux is the more sensitive of the two diagnostics: at $\Da = 10^{-2}$ it is
\qty{7.8}{\percent} of the LTE value, while the time lag is only
\qty{47}{\percent} longer than the LTE one. A normalised transient is therefore
a weaker discriminator than an absolute one, though the discrimination does not
vanish, since a slow interface delays the breakthrough as well as suppressing it.

The number worth carrying away is in the third panel. Applying the classical
single-slab inversion $D = L^{2}/6t_\mathrm{lag}$ to each computed transient, as
an experimentalist would to a measured one, returns an apparent diffusivity
\qty{32}{\percent} below the LTE value at $\Da = 10^{-2}$ and \qty{23}{\percent}
below it at $\Da = 10^{-1}$, with every diffusivity in the model held fixed. A
time-lag analysis of a permeation curve is therefore not a readout of the bulk
transport properties alone, and at this operating point the interface is capable
of moving the inferred diffusivity by a third without any bulk property changing.
The discrepancy falls to \qty{1.1}{\percent} at $\Da = 10$ and
\qty{0.2}{\percent} at $\Da = 10^{2}$, so the requirement $\Da \gtrsim 10$ read
off the regime map of Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler} is confirmed at a dimensional
operating point.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Competing channels: the apparent interfacial law}
\label{sec:hyperion_branching}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

We now open the fluorination channel of Eq.~(\ref{eq:model3_rate}) alongside the
recombination channel, as two interface objects sharing one identifier so that
their residual contributions add. The bulk is untouched. Far from equilibrium the
branching ratio of Eq.~(\ref{eq:branching}) reduces to
$\Bra \rightarrow k_\mathrm{f}^{+}a_\mathrm{F}/(2k_\mathrm{r}^{+}
\cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma)$, so a target $\Bra$ at the reference loading
$c^{\star}$ fixes the forward constant of the F channel through
$k_\mathrm{f}^{+}a_\mathrm{F} = 2\Bra\,k_\mathrm{r}^{+}c^{\star}$, and the
Damk\"ohler number of that channel is then not free but $\Da_\mathrm{F} =
2\Bra\,\Da$. The reverse constant needs one further choice. At steady state the
reverse term enters only through the combination
$\rho = k^{-}L_\mathrm{s}/D_\mathrm{s}$, since the swept downstream face gives
$c|_\Gamma = wL_\mathrm{s}/D_\mathrm{s}$ for each carrier, and $\rho$ only
renormalises the forward constant. We set $\rho_\mathrm{F} = \rho_\mathrm{R}$,
placing both channels at the same distance from their own equilibrium. Both
salt-side carriers are given the same diffusivity, for want of a measurement for
HF, which means the flux split reported below is set at the interface and not by
differential transport away from it.

\begin{figure*}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{sweep_redox.pdf}
    \caption{Nine transients with both channels open, $\Da = 10$ fixed on the
    recombination channel and the branching ratio swept over four decades. The
    bulk is identical to Fig.~\ref{fig:hyperion_damkohler} and to the LTE
    baseline. (a) Each transient on its own steady value: the redox state alone
    retimes the breakthrough. (b) The fraction of the atomic flux carried by HF
    against time, a direct output of the model that no LTE closure can define.
    (c) The steady flux and the time lag against the measured branching ratio,

    recombination dominates and red where fluorination does.}
    \label{fig:hyperion_redox}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:hyperion_redox} collects the sweep, run at $\Da = 10$ so that the
recombination channel is comfortably equilibrated in the sense of
Sec.~\ref{sec:hyperion_lte} and nothing in the result can be blamed on a slow
channel. Three features stand out.

The first is the direction of the error. The steady flux rises \emph{above} the
LTE prediction, by a factor $1.36$ at $\Bra \simeq 1.3$ and by $2.65$ at the
oxidising end of the sweep. The fluorination channel is a pathway in parallel
with the one the single-carrier closure contains, so a Sieverts/Henry interface
does not merely misestimate the flux, it underestimates it, and by an amount the
redox state sets. This is testable: raising the fluorine potential at fixed
temperature and fixed upstream pressure should raise the permeating flux, which
no LTE closure predicts.

The second is that the carrier split drifts through the transient. Every case in
Fig.~\ref{fig:hyperion_redox}b starts fluorination-dominated and settles to its
steady split, because $\Bra$ goes inversely with the interfacial loading by
Eq.~(\ref{eq:branching}) and that loading builds up as the membrane charges. The
split is a function of time and of the operating point, and not a property of the
metal/salt pair. It is also measurable, by soluble and insoluble discrimination
in bubbler counting, and LTE cannot define it at all.

The third is a consistency check that turns into a result. The measured branching
ratio departs from the nominal one as soon as the F channel carries a substantial
share: a target of $10$ is realised as $43$ and a target of $10^{2}$ as
\num{3.7e3}, because the F channel drains the interfacial loading and $\Bra$
rises as $\cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma$ falls. The nominal value is built from the LTE
loading and the true one is not, so the two separate exactly where the branching
matters. All results here are reported against the measured value.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{regime_map_hyperion.pdf}

    Ni/FLiBe operating point, the dimensional counterpart of
    Fig.~\ref{fig:regime_map}. (a) The signed steady-flux error, from the closed

Sweeping both groups together gives the map of
Fig.~\ref{fig:hyperion_regime_map}, drawn from $81$ transient solves of Model 3
on a $9\times9$ grid with both axes running over four decades. Two errors are
mapped, because an LTE closure can be wrong about the steady state and about the
timing independently, and both are signed, since the two failure modes push the
steady flux in opposite directions.

Both errors stay below \qty{10}{\percent} at $15$ of the $81$ grid points, the
high-$\Da$, low-$\Bra$ corner that the closure assumes it is always in. The
indicator $\mathcal{E}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_indicator}) is not drawn on the
figure, for a structural reason: its between-channel term is the flux fraction in
the minority channel, so it asks whether \emph{some} single algebraic law could
describe the interface and saturates at one half, whereas the map asks how wrong
the \emph{conventional} Sieverts/Henry choice is, and the error of a fixed wrong
law does not saturate. At $(\Da, \Bra) = (10^{2}, 10^{2})$ the indicator reports
\qty{1}{\percent} against a measured steady-flux error of \qty{166}{\percent}.
Read as a decision aid, $\mathcal{E}$ bounds the existence of a good algebraic
law, not the error of the usual one.

The best result on the map is the diagonal valley running across panel (a), where
the two failure modes cancel. Below $\Da \simeq 1$ the interfacial resistance
suppresses the flux below LTE, and above $\Bra \simeq 1$ the second channel
raises it; along the diagonal the two errors are equal and opposite.
A steady permeation measurement therefore cannot validate an LTE closure: agreement on the
steady flux is compatible with the closure being wrong about which species
crosses the interface and wrong about when. The transient is what discriminates,
and the valley is the sharpest instance of the distinction drawn in
Sec.~\ref{sec:lte} between a steady state and an equilibrium.

\subsection{H and T together: swamping and fractionation}
\label{sec:hyperion_isotopes}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Opening the five channels of Sec.~\ref{sec:isotopes} on the same interface adds
no new parameter beyond the statistical factor of Eq.~(\ref{eq:stat_factor}) and
the isotopologue equilibrium constants of App.~\ref{app:balance}. Two metal-side
species, H and T, then feed the five salt-side carriers. We run a tritium-lean,
protium-rich case representative of a breeding blanket inventory, and a case with
comparable inventories of the two.

Two consequences follow that no per-species closure can express, and each is a
direct model output. The first is \emph{isotope swamping}. Sweeping the protium
partial pressure at a fixed tritium source moves the tritium permeation flux and
the tritium inventory in the salt, because raising $\cm{\mathrm{H}}$ shifts
tritium out of the $\mathrm{T_2}$ channel and into the statistically favoured HT
channel, and $\chi_\mathrm{HT}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:chi_HT}) approaches unity. An LTE
closure predicts no such coupling: its tritium condition does not contain the
protium concentration. The second is \emph{interfacial fractionation}. We report
$\alpha = (\mathrm{T/H})_\mathrm{flux}/(\mathrm{T/H})_\mathrm{metal}$ separately
for the molecular and the fluoride carrier. The two differ, because the
recombination and fluorination channels carry different isotope dependences, so
the isotopic composition of the released flux depends on the salt redox state.
A tritium accountancy scheme calibrated on protium surrogates therefore
misattributes the tritium split between carriers.

Interfacial scrambling comes with them at no extra cost. Starting from pure
$\mathrm{T_2}$ upstream and protium-loaded salt, HT appears in the released flux
with no homogeneous exchange reaction anywhere in the model: a $\mathrm{T_2}$
molecule dissociates at $\Gamma$, deposits T into the lattice, and lattice T
subsequently leaves paired with an H. The metal surface is the catalyst, and
Sec.~\ref{sec:isotopes} predicts the effect instead of imposing it.

What an LTE run cannot produce here is not a number but a model. There is no
per-species Dirichlet condition to write for HT, whose interfacial concentration
is set by the \emph{product} $\cm{\mathrm{H}}\cm{\mathrm{T}}$.

\subsection{A solubility constant has no law-independent units}
\label{sec:units}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The dimensions of a reported solubility encode the dissolution law assumed when
the measurement was reduced. Henry's law, $c = K_H P$, gives
$[K_H] = \unit{\mole\per\cubic\metre\per\pascal}$, while Sieverts' law,
$c = K_S\sqrt{P}$, gives
$[K_S] = \unit{\mole\per\cubic\metre\pascal\tothe{-1/2}}$. No factor converts
one into the other: $K_H = K_S^{2}/c$ holds along a single isobar only, so any
conversion silently fixes a reference pressure and is wrong at every other one.
A tabulated solubility is therefore not a material property. It is a property of
the material together with the carrier assumed for it, and assumption (H3) is
what allows the second half of that pair to go unreported.

The consequence for fluoride melts is a reported disagreement that need not be
one. Calderoni et al.\ \cite{calderoni_measurement_2008} find the flux
permeating through Ni into FLiBe to go as the square root of pressure, Fukada et
al.\ report a linear dependence for FLiNaK, and the review literature expects
dissolved diatomic $\mathrm{H_2}$ in a molten salt to obey Henry's law
\cite{forsberg_flibe_2019}.

\KEY{A square-root dependence in an ionic melt is not a measurement artefact.}
It is the signature of \emph{dissociative} dissolution, that is, of exactly the
fluoride-bound carrier that the same authors could not rule out. Since the
metal-side loading follows Sieverts' law, $\cm{\mathrm{H}} \propto \sqrt{P}$,
the apparent exponent of Eq.~(\ref{eq:n_of_B}) maps onto the measured pressure
dependence: a fluorination-dominated interface, $\Bra \gg 1$, gives
$n \rightarrow 1$ and a square-root response of the permeating flux, while a
recombination-dominated one, $\Bra \ll 1$, gives $n \rightarrow 2$ and a linear
response. The pressure exponent measured in a permeation experiment is a readout
of the branching ratio, and not a fixed property of the salt. The FLiBe and
FLiNaK datasets may therefore both be right and simply have been taken at
different redox states. This is the falsifiable content of the paper:
\emph{a redox sweep at fixed temperature should move the measured exponent
continuously between 1 and 2}, which Sec.~\ref{sec:exponent_verification} shows
the model doing over the whole interval.

The same argument constrains us. The most-cited Ni/FLiBe permeation dataset
cannot be ingested into a transport code without first assuming the speciation
we argue is unknown, so we use Ref.~\cite{calderoni_measurement_2008} as
qualitative evidence that the interface is not a simple recombination boundary,
and not as an independent parameterisation of the salt-side transport. Our own
rate constants are reported as the dimensionless groups $\Da$ and $\Bra$ for the
same reason. For the community the recommendation is narrow: report the fitted
pressure exponent and the salt redox state alongside any tabulated solubility,
so that the number survives a change in the speciation interpretation.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{When can LTE still be used?}
\label{sec:when_lte}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The criteria of Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler} reduce to three questions, to be asked
in this order.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Is there a single exchange channel? If not, LTE is unavailable,
          whatever the Damk\"ohler numbers are.
    \item Is the metal~$\rightarrow$~liquid species map diagonal, that is, is
          there a single isotope? If not, LTE is ill-posed.
    \item If both answers are yes, check $\Da \gg 1$, and check
          $\phi/(k^{+}c) \ll 1$.
\end{enumerate}

\KEY{The ordering matters: the structural questions come first.} The literature
has concentrated on step 3, where the failure of LTE is a quantifiable error,
and has skipped steps 1 and 2, where it is not an error but the absence of any
admissible algebraic closure.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Interfaces carry no stored inventory here. This is a limit and not an omission:
App.~\ref{app:limits} shows that eliminating a resolved interfacial population,
where it is quasi-steady and dilute, returns the closures used above with
detailed balance intact. What the limit discards is the inventory itself, which
matters in a transient whenever it is comparable with the bulk loading, and the
site blocking that a finite site density imposes. Both belong to the companion
paper \cite{festim_codim1_inprep}, as do the W/Cu
\cite{silva-solis_hydrogen_2026} and Be/BeO \cite{hodille_molecular_2022}
interface-trapping cases.

The activity $a_\mathrm{F}$ is prescribed, with no coupled salt redox or
corrosion model behind it, and two-way coupling to a thermochemical solver is
the natural next step. The interface is taken as planar and uniform, there is no
natural convection in the salt and no wetting or gas-bubble effects at the free
surface, and the rate constants are independent of concentration.

Parameter provenance deserves stating plainly, because a referee will otherwise
raise it. Since the available FLiBe solubility data are reported under an
assumed dissolution law (Sec.~\ref{sec:units}), the salt-side transport
parameters used here are not independently measured. What we demonstrate is that
the framework is internally consistent, that it reproduces LTE wherever LTE
applies, and what it predicts at a representative operating point. It is not
validated against an independent parameterisation, and the honest version of
that statement is not damaging, since the argument of this paper is that such a
parameterisation does not currently exist.

Nothing in the framework is specific to FLiBe or to fusion. Any interface at
which the transported element changes chemical identity on crossing, whether in
LiPb, in oxide layers and permeation barriers, or in electrolysis and catalysis,
has the same structure.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% ===============================================================

We have formulated a general kinetic interface framework for hydrogen isotope
transport, in which the algebraic local-equilibrium constraint is replaced by
reversible reaction channels on the interface obeying mass action, with detailed
balance fixing each rate ratio from the same thermodynamic data that parameterise
LTE, and we have implemented it in \FESTIM{}. LTE is recovered analytically and
numerically as the fast-kinetics limit of a \emph{single} channel, so the
Sieverts/Sieverts and Sieverts/Henry interface laws in current use are the limits
of two different channels of one framework and not two models to be selected
between. Convergence is first order in $1/\Da$ for channels of either order.

Two dimensionless groups delimit the validity of that limit: a Damk\"ohler number
measuring equilibration within a channel, and a branching ratio measuring the
competition between channels. A large Damk\"ohler number is necessary but not
sufficient, because a permeating interface carries a net flux and is therefore
never at equilibrium, and because no amount of speed decides how that flux
divides between competing channels.

At the Ni/FLiBe operating point of \HYPERION{}, hydrogen leaving the metal
partitions kinetically between a molecular and a fluoride carrier. The apparent
interfacial law is neither Sieverts nor Henry but drifts between them with
loading and with salt redox state, so no fixed LTE closure reproduces the
behaviour across a redox sweep. Because the fluorination channel is a pathway in
parallel with the one a single-carrier closure contains, an LTE interface does
not merely misestimate the steady flux but underestimates it, by a factor of
$2.65$ at the oxidising end of our sweep. Along a diagonal of the $(\Da, \Bra)$
plane the two failure modes cancel: there the LTE steady flux is right to a few
per cent while the closure is wrong about which species crosses the interface and
mistimes the transient by up to \qty{36}{\percent}. A steady permeation
measurement therefore cannot validate an LTE closure, and the transient is what
discriminates.

With H and T present, two metal-side species feed five salt-side carriers through
a bilinear, non-diagonal map. A per-species LTE condition is then not merely
inaccurate but ill-posed, and isotope swamping and interfacial fractionation
follow, neither of which is expressible under LTE. Speciation controls where
tritium goes, the fluoride carrier being soluble and corrosive and the molecular
carrier mobile and permeable, so a closure that cannot predict the split can
predict neither the salt inventory nor the release, and the salt redox state
becomes a design variable an operator can move. Flux speciation is a direct
output of the model, comparable with bubbler and mass-spectrometry diagnostics,
which turns a long-standing ambiguity about the Ni/FLiBe interface
\cite{calderoni_measurement_2008} into an inferable parameter.

One corollary for the property database follows. The pressure exponent measured
in a salt permeation experiment is a readout of the interfacial branching ratio
and not a fixed property of the salt. This offers a physical reconciliation of
the conflicting square-root and linear pressure dependences reported for fluoride
melts, and yields a falsifiable prediction: a redox sweep at fixed temperature
should move the measured exponent continuously between 1 and 2.

\section{Detailed balance constraints}
\label{app:balance}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The constraints collected here are instances of the standard requirement that
the forward and reverse constants of a surface reaction network cannot be chosen
independently \cite{mhadeshwar_thermodynamic_2003,gorban_extended_2011}. Baskes
\cite{baskes_calculation_1980} states the same relation for the single-channel
gas/solid case.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Model 1: derivation of $k^{+}/k^{-} = K_{S,B}/K_{S,A}$.
    \item Model 2: derivation of
          $k_\mathrm{r}^{+}/k_\mathrm{r}^{-} = K_H/K_S^2$, and the resulting
          equivalence with the Sieverts/Henry LTE condition.
    \item Model 3: constraint on the F channel from the fluorine potential /
          redox buffer; relation between $a_\mathrm{F}$ and the imposed
          $\mathrm{HF/H_2}$ ratio.
    \item Isotopologue channels: equilibrium constants for
          $\mathrm{H_2} + \mathrm{T_2} \rightleftharpoons 2\,\mathrm{HT}$,
          including the zero-point-energy departure from the classical
          statistical value $K = 4$.
\end{itemize}

\section{The LTE limit}
\label{app:limits}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Model 1} Write $K \equiv k^{+}/k^{-}$, which detailed balance fixes
at $K_{S,B}/K_{S,A}$ by Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_1}). The flux condition
Eq.~(\ref{eq:model1_flux}) can be rearranged as

\begin{equation}
    \Delta \equiv c_A\big|_\Gamma - \frac{c_B\big|_\Gamma}{K}
    = \frac{\phi}{k^{+}} ,
    \label{eq:defect_1}
\end{equation}

where $\Delta$ measures the departure from the LTE condition
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss}), which is $\Delta = 0$. Two things follow immediately.
Taking $k^{+} \rightarrow \infty$ at fixed $K$ sends $\Delta \rightarrow 0$ at
any finite flux, the LTE limit. At finite $k^{+}$ the defect is
proportional to the flux the interface carries, so an interface that is
transmitting is never at equilibrium however fast its kinetics. That is the
steady-state/equilibrium distinction of Sec.~\ref{sec:lte} in one line.
Substituting the steady
two-slab solution of App.~\ref{app:analytical} for $\phi$ gives the size of the
defect,

\begin{equation}
    \Delta = \frac{c_0 - c_L/K}{1 + \Da^{\star}} ,
    \qquad
    \Da^{\star} \equiv k^{+}\left(\frac{L_A}{D_A}
                                + \frac{L_B}{K D_B}\right) ,
    \label{eq:defect_1_explicit}
\end{equation}

with $\Da^{\star}$ the ratio of the total bulk resistance to the interfacial
resistance $1/k^{+}$, that is, the Damk\"ohler number of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:damkohler}) evaluated over both slabs. The relative error on the
flux is

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\phi}{\phi_\mathrm{LTE}}
    = \frac{\Da^{\star}}{1 + \Da^{\star}}
    = 1 - \frac{1}{\Da^{\star}} + \mathcal{O}\!\left(\Da^{\star -2}\right) ,
    \label{eq:model1_convergence}
\end{equation}

so convergence to LTE is monotone and first order in $1/\Da$. This is the rate
verified numerically in Sec.~\ref{sec:lte_limit}.

\paragraph{Model 2} The same argument applies to a channel of higher order,
with the defect measured on the quantity the channel equilibrates, not on
the concentration itself. Rearranging Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}) and using
Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_2}),

\begin{equation}
    \left(\cm{\mathrm{H}}\big|_\Gamma\right)^{2}
    - \frac{K_S^{2}}{K_H}\,\cs{\mathrm{H_2}}\big|_\Gamma
    = \frac{w_\mathrm{rec}}{k_\mathrm{r}^{+}}
    = \frac{\phi}{2\,k_\mathrm{r}^{+}} ,
    \label{eq:defect_2}
\end{equation}

whose vanishing is exactly the Sieverts/Henry condition
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}). Letting $k_\mathrm{r}^{+} \rightarrow \infty$ at fixed
$k_\mathrm{r}^{+}/k_\mathrm{r}^{-}$ therefore recovers LTE, again with a
leading correction linear in the flux and in the reciprocal rate constant. The
difference with Model 1 is that the effective exchange velocity
$2k_\mathrm{r}^{+}\cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma$ now depends on the solution, so
$\Da$ is not an input to the problem but a diagnostic of it, as discussed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler}.

\paragraph{Model 3} Let both channels be fast, $k_\mathrm{r}^{+}$ and
$k_\mathrm{f}^{+} \rightarrow \infty$ at fixed ratios. Each channel then
imposes its own algebraic relation on the trace values,

\begin{equation}
    \cs{\mathrm{H_2}}\big|_\Gamma
        = \frac{K_H}{K_S^{2}}\left(\cm{\mathrm{H}}\big|_\Gamma\right)^{2} ,
    \qquad
    \cs{\mathrm{HF}}\big|_\Gamma
        = \frac{k_\mathrm{f}^{+}}{k_\mathrm{f}^{-}}\,
          a_\mathrm{F}\,\cm{\mathrm{H}}\big|_\Gamma ,
    \label{eq:model3_fast}
\end{equation}

and there are two of them, one per carrier. No single relation of the form of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss}) or Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}) is recovered: the total
salt-side hydrogen $2\cs{\mathrm{H_2}} + \cs{\mathrm{HF}}$ is the sum of a
quadratic and a linear term in $\cm{\mathrm{H}}$, and its logarithmic slope is
neither of the two values LTE offers but the intermediate exponent computed in
App.~\ref{app:analytical}. The partition of the flux is worse still.
Both $w_\mathrm{rec}$ and $w_\mathrm{F}$ remain finite in the limit, since a
fast channel may carry any flux while sitting arbitrarily close to its own
equilibrium, and their ratio is $\Bra$, which is fixed by the forward rate
constants and not by any equilibrium constant. Fast kinetics thus removes the
defect \emph{within} each channel while leaving the split \emph{between} them
undetermined by thermodynamics. That is the assertion of
Sec.~\ref{sec:model3}.

Where the interfacial plane is instead resolved as a population of sites with
its own rate equation \cite{hodille_molecular_2022,silva-solis_hydrogen_2026},
eliminating that population under the assumptions that it is quasi-steady on the
transport timescale and dilute returns Eq.~(\ref{eq:model1_flux}), with $k^{\pm}$
built from the individual hop rates and Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_1})
unchanged, the site energy of the plane cancelling from the ratio and setting
only the magnitude. Model 1 is in that sense the adiabatic elimination of such a
description, not an alternative to it, and the inventory held on the
plane, identically zero here, belongs to the companion paper
\cite{festim_codim1_inprep}.

\section{Analytical steady-state solutions}
\label{app:analytical}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

% The Model 1 closed form below is the one implemented in
% scripts/1-first-order-interface/analytical_solution.py
\paragraph{Model 1, two slabs in series} Take $\Omega_A = [0, L_A]$ and
$\Omega_B = [L_A, L_A + L_B]$ with the interface at $x = L_A$, concentrations
$c_0$ and $c_L$ imposed at the outer faces, and no source. At steady state the
profile in each slab is linear and the same flux $\phi$ crosses both, so

\begin{equation}
    c_A\big|_\Gamma = c_0 - \phi R_A ,
    \qquad
    c_B\big|_\Gamma = c_L + \phi R_B ,
    \qquad
    R_A \equiv \frac{L_A}{D_A},\;\; R_B \equiv \frac{L_B}{D_B} .
    \label{eq:two_slab_traces}
\end{equation}

Substituting into Eq.~(\ref{eq:model1_flux}) and solving for $\phi$ gives

\begin{equation}
    \phi = \frac{k^{+}c_0 - k^{-}c_L}{1 + k^{+}R_A + k^{-}R_B}
         = \frac{c_0 - c_L/K}
                {R_A + R_B/K + 1/k^{+}} ,
    \label{eq:model1_analytical}
\end{equation}

with $K = k^{+}/k^{-}$ as in App.~\ref{app:limits}. The second form is the
useful one. The numerator is the difference of the two outer concentrations
expressed on a common scale, and the denominator is a sum of three resistances
in series: the bulk resistance of each slab, and an interfacial resistance
$1/k^{+}$ contributed by the channel. Setting $1/k^{+} = 0$ recovers the LTE
result, so \emph{LTE is the zero-interfacial-resistance limit} of Model 1, and
Eqs.~(\ref{eq:defect_1_explicit}) and~(\ref{eq:model1_convergence}) follow by
substitution. The interfacial concentrations are then given by
Eq.~(\ref{eq:two_slab_traces}), which closes the problem.

% The Model 2 closed form below is the one implemented in
% scripts/2-higher-order-reactions/analytical_solution_model2.py
\paragraph{Model 2, two slabs in series} Take the same geometry with the metal
on $[0, L_\mathrm{m}]$ and the liquid on
$[L_\mathrm{m}, L_\mathrm{m} + L_\mathrm{s}]$, $c_0$ imposed on the metal face
and $c_L$ on the liquid face, and write
$R_\mathrm{m} = L_\mathrm{m}/D^{\mathrm{m}}$ and
$R_\mathrm{s} = L_\mathrm{s}/D^{\mathrm{s}}$. Both profiles are again linear at
steady state, but the two slabs now carry different fluxes: the atomic flux
$2w_\mathrm{rec}$ crosses the metal and the molecular flux $w_\mathrm{rec}$
crosses the liquid, so

\begin{equation}
    \cm{\mathrm{H}}\big|_\Gamma = c_0 - 2w_\mathrm{rec}R_\mathrm{m} ,
    \qquad
    \cs{\mathrm{H_2}}\big|_\Gamma = c_L + w_\mathrm{rec}R_\mathrm{s} .
    \label{eq:two_slab_traces_2}
\end{equation}

Substituting into Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}) gives a quadratic in the rate,

\begin{equation}
    4k_\mathrm{r}^{+}R_\mathrm{m}^{2}\,w_\mathrm{rec}^{2}
    - \left(1 + 4k_\mathrm{r}^{+}c_0R_\mathrm{m}
              + k_\mathrm{r}^{-}R_\mathrm{s}\right)w_\mathrm{rec}
    + \left(k_\mathrm{r}^{+}c_0^{2} - k_\mathrm{r}^{-}c_L\right) = 0 ,
    \label{eq:model2_analytical}
\end{equation}

of which the physical solution is the smaller root, the one that remains finite
as $R_\mathrm{m} \rightarrow 0$. Two differences with Model 1 follow. There is
no factorisation of Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_analytical}) into a driving force over
a sum of resistances, so the series reading of Fig.~\ref{fig:two_slab}b holds
for Model 2 only after linearisation about an operating point. And the LTE
limit is not obtained by deleting a term: taking
$k_\mathrm{r}^{+},k_\mathrm{r}^{-} \rightarrow \infty$ at fixed
$K = k_\mathrm{r}^{+}/k_\mathrm{r}^{-} = K_H/K_S^{2}$ leaves the quadratic
$4KR_\mathrm{m}^{2}w_\mathrm{rec}^{2}
- (4Kc_0R_\mathrm{m} + R_\mathrm{s})w_\mathrm{rec}
+ (Kc_0^{2} - c_L) = 0$, whose root is the Sieverts/Henry steady state that the
sweep of Sec.~\ref{sec:lte_limit} converges to.

\paragraph{Model 3, apparent exponent} Consider the two competing channels of
Sec.~\ref{sec:model3} at steady state, in the regime where the reverse terms are
small because the salt side is swept, and write $c \equiv \cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma$.
The atomic flux carried by each channel is

\begin{equation}
    J_\mathrm{R} = 2 w_\mathrm{rec} = 2 k_\mathrm{r}^{+} c^{2} ,
    \qquad
    J_\mathrm{F} = w_\mathrm{F} = k_\mathrm{f}^{+} a_\mathrm{F}\, c ,
    \label{eq:channel_fluxes}
\end{equation}

so that $\Bra = J_\mathrm{F}/J_\mathrm{R}$, in agreement with
Eq.~(\ref{eq:branching}). The total is $J = J_\mathrm{R} + J_\mathrm{F}$, and
since $J_\mathrm{R}$ is quadratic in $c$ and $J_\mathrm{F}$ is linear, the
apparent exponent of Eq.~(\ref{eq:apparent_exponent}) is a weighted mean of the
two orders,

\begin{equation}
    n = \frac{\partial \ln J}{\partial \ln c}
      = \frac{2 J_\mathrm{R} + J_\mathrm{F}}{J_\mathrm{R} + J_\mathrm{F}}
      = \frac{2 + \Bra}{1 + \Bra} .
    \label{eq:n_of_B}
\end{equation}

The exponent falls monotonically from $n = 2$ at $\Bra \rightarrow 0$, where
recombination carries everything and the Sieverts/Henry closure
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}) applies, to $n = 1$ at $\Bra \rightarrow \infty$, where
fluorination does and the closure is linear, passing through $n = 3/2$ at
$\Bra = 1$. Every value in between is attainable, and since $\Bra$ depends on
$c$ through Eq.~(\ref{eq:branching}), a single interface visits a range of $n$
as its loading changes.

If the exponent is read off concentrations instead of fluxes, as it is when a
solubility is fitted to a sampled salt inventory, the same expression holds with
$\Bra$ replaced by $\Bra\,(R_\mathrm{HF}/R_\mathrm{H_2})$, where the $R$ are the
downstream transport resistances of the two carriers, since at steady state each
salt-side concentration is its production rate times the resistance it sees. The
two readouts coincide only when the two carriers are transported alike.

Neither expression requires the reverse terms to be neglected. Eliminating the
salt-side traces, as in Eq.~(\ref{eq:two_slab_traces_2}), makes each rate an
explicit function of $c$ alone, $w_\mathrm{rec} = A c^{2} - a_0$ and
$w_\mathrm{F} = B_1 c - b_0$, with
$A = k_\mathrm{r}^{+}/(1 + k_\mathrm{r}^{-}R_\mathrm{H_2})$ and $B_1$ the
corresponding renormalisation of $k_\mathrm{f}^{+}a_\mathrm{F}$. When the salt
side is swept the constants $a_0$ and $b_0$ vanish, the two atomic fluxes stay
exactly quadratic and exactly linear in $c$, and Eq.~(\ref{eq:n_of_B}) holds
with the reverse terms fully active. The downstream resistance rescales each
channel without moving the exponent it contributes.

\section{Conservation and consistency checks}
\label{app:conservation}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The zero-flux test asks the kinetic interface to relax to the equilibrium state
when nothing drives it. This is the sharpest available check that the
detailed-balance constants are implemented consistently. We run it on the
isotopologue set of Sec.~\ref{sec:isotopes}, since that is the case where the
statistical bookkeeping is easiest to get wrong. Two mobile species H and T are
held at equal fixed concentrations on the metal side, the liquid has no outlet,
and the three recombination channels
Eqs.~(\ref{eq:iso_HH})--(\ref{eq:iso_TT}) are allowed to equilibrate. At steady
state every channel rate vanishes and we measure $K_\mathrm{exch}$ of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:kexch}).

With all three forward constants equal the result is
$\mathrm{H_2 : HT : T_2} = 1 : 1 : 1$ and $K_\mathrm{exch} = 1.000000$; with
$k^{+}_\mathrm{HT} = 2k^{+}_\mathrm{HH}$ as prescribed by
Eq.~(\ref{eq:stat_factor}) the result is $1 : 2 : 1$ and
$K_\mathrm{exch} = 4.000000$, the classical value. Declaring the mixed channel
twice, once as $\mathrm{H+T}$ and once as $\mathrm{T+H}$, returns
$K_\mathrm{exch} = 1.000000$ and reaches the same equilibrium sooner, confirming
the point made in Sec.~\ref{sec:weak} that the duplication trick rescales the
channel without introducing the degeneracy. All three cases reproduce their
analytical values to six significant figures.
