\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ===============================================================

\begin{itemize}
    \item Hydrogen isotope transport controls fuel retention, permeation losses
          and tritium accountancy in fusion systems. In liquid-breeder concepts
          the tritium path crosses at least one metal/liquid interface before
          reaching an extraction or release point.

    \item Macroscopic transport codes (\FESTIM{} \cite{dark_festim_2026},
          TMAP8 \cite{simon_moose-based_2025},
          \MHIMS{} \cite{hodille_macroscopic_2015}) close such interfaces with
          local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE), i.e.\ continuity of the
          chemical potential of the transported species. In practice this is
          imposed as an algebraic (Dirichlet-type) constraint relating the two
          interfacial concentrations:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Sieverts/Sieverts: $c_A/K_{S,A} = c_B/K_{S,B}$
              \item Sieverts/Henry: $c_B = K_{H,B}\,(c_A/K_{S,A})^2$
          \end{itemize}

    \item Three assumptions are hidden in that closure, and they are rarely
          stated together:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item[(H1)] interfacial equilibration is fast compared with bulk
                    transport;
              \item[(H2)] a \emph{single} exchange pathway connects the two sides;
              \item[(H3)] the identity of the carrier species on each side is
                    known a priori and fixed, so that a single solubility law
                    applies.
          \end{itemize}

    \item (H1) is the assumption usually scrutinised, and it is known to fail:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Hodille et al.\ \cite{hodille_molecular_2022}: MD at the
                    Be/BeO interface shows LTE is not reached even at 1500~K.
              \item Silva-Sol\'is et al.\ \cite{silva-solis_hydrogen_2026}:
                    DFT+MRE for W/Cu shows the steady state is not the
                    thermodynamic equilibrium state whenever a net flux is
                    carried.
          \end{itemize}

    \item \KEY{(H2) and (H3) have received almost no attention, and this paper
          argues they are the more consequential failures.} The motivating
          system is a metal in contact with a fluoride melt.

    \item Metal/molten-salt interfaces: hydrogen leaving the metal lattice has
          more than one chemical fate. It may recombine to $\mathrm{H_2}$ and
          dissolve physically (Henry's law, low solubility, high mobility), or
          it may be oxidised by the melt to HF and dissolve chemically (much
          higher solubility, corrosive, redox dependent). The branching between
          the two is kinetic and depends on the salt redox state.

    \item This ambiguity is not hypothetical. Calderoni et al.\
          \cite{calderoni_measurement_2008}, measuring T permeation through a Ni
          membrane into FLiBe, concluded that tritium absorbed atomically in the
          nickel does \emph{not} simply recombine at the Ni/FLiBe interface, and
          could not distinguish between transport as T bound to
          $\mathrm{BeF_4^-}$, as HT, or both. Nearly two decades later the
          speciation at that interface is still an open question, and reported
          FLiBe transport properties for $\mathrm{H_2}$/HF and their
          isotopologues still span orders of magnitude \cite{libra_2022}.

    \item The ambiguity has already contaminated the data record. The solubility
          constant reported in \cite{calderoni_measurement_2008} carries units
          inconsistent with the dissolution law it is routinely used with
          downstream (Sec.~\ref{sec:units}). This is not a typographical slip:
          \emph{a solubility constant has no law-independent units}.
          Sieverts-type dissolution gives
          $\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}\,Pa^{-1/2}}$, Henry-type gives
          $\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}\,Pa^{-1}}$, and the two cannot be interconverted
          by any fixed factor. Reporting a number therefore presupposes the
          speciation --- exactly the quantity in question. We treat this as a
          motivating symptom rather than a criticism of the measurement: it is
          what assumption (H3) looks like when it fails in practice.

    \item It gets structurally worse with more than one isotope. Two mobile
          atomic species in the metal (H, T) feed five carriers in the salt
          ($\mathrm{H_2}$, HT, $\mathrm{T_2}$, HF, TF). The map is quadratic and
          non-diagonal: $c_\mathrm{HT}$ depends on the \emph{product} of the two
          metal-side concentrations. No per-species chemical-potential
          continuity condition can express this --- LTE here is not inaccurate,
          it is ill-posed.

    \item Gap: no macroscopic hydrogen transport code implements a general
          kinetic interface condition with competing reaction channels and
          multiple isotopologues.

    \item This work:
          \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
              \item formulates a general kinetic interface framework based on
                    reversible reaction channels and mass action, with detailed
                    balance fixing rate ratios from thermodynamics;
              \item implements it in \FESTIM{} \cite{dark_festim_2026};
              \item recovers LTE analytically as the fast-kinetics
                    single-channel limit, in both Sieverts/Sieverts and
                    Sieverts/Henry forms;
              \item derives a Damk\"ohler criterion (within a channel) and a
                    branching ratio (between channels) delimiting LTE validity;
              \item applies the framework to the \HYPERION{} Ni/FLiBe/gas
                    experiment \cite{hyperion_facility}, in single-isotope and
                    H/T modes.
          \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Scope note.} Interfaces are treated here as reactive surfaces
          without their own stored inventory. Intermediate trapped states
          localised on the interface --- and the codimension-1 formulation
          needed to carry an interfacial ODE alongside the bulk PDEs --- are the
          subject of a companion paper \cite{festim_codim1_inprep}. The Be/BeO
          and W/Cu interface-trapping cases of
          Refs.~\cite{hodille_molecular_2022,silva-solis_hydrogen_2026} belong
          there.

\section{Interface conditions for hydrogen transport}
\label{sec:models}
% ===============================================================

Throughout this section we consider two subdomains $\Omega_A$ and $\Omega_B$
sharing an interface $\Gamma$. In each subdomain the transport of a dissolved
species obeys the usual diffusion equation
$\partial_t c = \nabla\!\cdot\!(D\nabla c) + S$ solved by \FESTIM{}
\cite{dark_festim_2026}; trapping is omitted for clarity and plays no part in
the arguments that follow. What distinguishes the models discussed below is
solely how the two bulk problems are coupled across $\Gamma$.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Local thermodynamic equilibrium and its assumptions}
\label{sec:lte}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE) is the statement that the chemical
potential of dissolved hydrogen is continuous across $\Gamma$. Since the
relation between chemical potential and concentration depends on the
dissolution law obeyed on each side, the resulting algebraic condition takes a
different form depending on which pair of laws applies. For two materials in
which hydrogen dissolves dissociatively (Sieverts' law on both sides) it reads

\begin{equation}
    \frac{c_A\big|_\Gamma}{K_{S,A}(T)}
    = \frac{c_B\big|_\Gamma}{K_{S,B}(T)}
    \qquad \text{(Sieverts / Sieverts)}
    \label{eq:lte_ss}
\end{equation}

whereas for a solid in contact with a liquid in which hydrogen dissolves
molecularly (Henry's law) it becomes

\begin{equation}
    c_B\big|_\Gamma
    = K_{H,B}(T)\left(\frac{c_A\big|_\Gamma}{K_{S,A}(T)}\right)^{2}
    \qquad \text{(Sieverts / Henry)} .
    \label{eq:lte_sh}
\end{equation}

Both conditions are \emph{algebraic}: they fix a ratio, or a power law, between
the two interfacial concentrations independently of the flux the interface is
carrying. Three consequences follow, and they are properties of the closure
rather than of the physical system it is applied to. First, the interface has
no timescale of its own: it responds instantaneously to any change in the
adjacent bulk fields. Second, it offers no resistance, so $\Gamma$ can never be
the rate-limiting step of a permeation problem. Third, the exponent
distinguishing Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss}) from Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}), and hence
the identity of the carrier species on the $B$ side, must be chosen before the
simulation is run.

There is also a formal difficulty, following
Silva-Sol\'is et al.\ \cite{silva-solis_hydrogen_2026}. Thermodynamic
equilibrium requires every microscopic forward rate to be balanced by its
reverse, which implies \emph{zero} net flux across $\Gamma$. A permeation
experiment, however, carries a nonzero net flux by construction. Imposing LTE
at a permeating interface is therefore always an approximation; the only
question is how large the error is, and Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler} makes that
question quantitative.

Two notions are routinely conflated and are kept distinct throughout.
A \emph{steady state} satisfies $\partial_t c = 0$ with
$\Gamma_\mathrm{in} = \Gamma_\mathrm{out} \neq 0$, whereas \emph{equilibrium}
satisfies $\partial_t c = 0$ with
$\Gamma_\mathrm{in} = \Gamma_\mathrm{out} = 0$. Equilibrium is a steady state;
the converse is false, and it is precisely the difference between the two that
LTE discards.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{A general kinetic interface framework}
\label{sec:general}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

We replace the algebraic constraint by a set of reversible reaction channels
living on $\Gamma$. Let $X_i$ denote the mobile atomic species on side $A$
($i \in \{\mathrm{H,D,T}\}$) and $Y_\alpha$ the carriers on side $B$
($\alpha \in \{\mathrm{H_2, HT, T_2, HF, TF},\dots\}$). A channel $r$ is written

\begin{equation}
    \sum_i \nu_{ir} X_i + \sum_\beta \lambda_{\beta r} Z_\beta
    \;\rightleftharpoons\; \sum_\alpha \mu_{\alpha r} Y_\alpha ,
    \label{eq:channel}
\end{equation}

where the $Z_\beta$ are non-hydrogenic constituents entering the stoichiometry
(the fluoride ion and the oxidising or reducing half of the salt redox buffer,
in the application of Sec.~\ref{sec:model3}). They are represented by
activities $a_\beta$ rather than by transported fields. Each channel proceeds
at a net rate given by mass action,

\begin{equation}
    w_r = k_r^{+}(T)\prod_i \left(\cm{i}\big|_\Gamma\right)^{\nu_{ir}}
                     \prod_\beta a_\beta^{\lambda_{\beta r}}
        - k_r^{-}(T)\prod_\alpha \left(\cs{\alpha}\big|_\Gamma\right)^{\mu_{\alpha r}} ,
    \label{eq:mass_action}
\end{equation}

expressed per unit interfacial area, so that $w_r$ has units of
$\mathrm{mol\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$ and the units of $k_r^{\pm}$ depend on the order
of the channel: an exchange velocity in $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ for a first-order
channel, $\mathrm{m^4\,mol^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$ for a second-order one. The interface
conditions are then the statement that the net atomic flux of isotope $i$
leaving $A$, and the production of carrier $\alpha$ into $B$, are sums over all
channels, $\mathbf{n}$ denoting throughout the outward normal of the subdomain
whose equation is being written:

\begin{align}
    -D^{\mathrm{m}}_i \nabla \cm{i}\cdot \mathbf{n}\big|_\Gamma
        &= \sum_r \nu_{ir}\, w_r
    \label{eq:bc_metal}\\
     D^{\mathrm{s}}_\alpha \nabla \cs{\alpha}\cdot \mathbf{n}\big|_\Gamma
        &= \sum_r \mu_{\alpha r}\, w_r .
    \label{eq:bc_salt}
\end{align}

Conservation of atoms across $\Gamma$ is not an additional requirement but a
condition on the stoichiometric coefficients. Writing $n_{i\alpha}$ for the
number of atoms of isotope $i$ carried by species $\alpha$, every channel must
satisfy

\begin{equation}
    \nu_{ir} = \sum_\alpha \mu_{\alpha r}\, n_{i\alpha}
    \qquad \text{for each isotope } i ,
    \label{eq:atom_balance}
\end{equation}

so that the atomic flux removed from $A$ by Eq.~(\ref{eq:bc_metal}) is exactly
the atomic flux delivered to $B$ by Eq.~(\ref{eq:bc_salt}). For the
recombination channel of Sec.~\ref{sec:model2}, for instance,
$\nu_\mathrm{H} = 2$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{H_2}} = 1$ with
$n_{\mathrm{H,H_2}} = 2$.

The forward and reverse rate constants of each channel are not independent.
Detailed balance requires

\begin{equation}
    \frac{k_r^{+}(T)}{k_r^{-}(T)} = K_r(T) ,
    \label{eq:detailed_balance_general}
\end{equation}

with $K_r$ the equilibrium constant of that channel, obtained from the
solubilities of the participating species and the relevant formation free
energies. Thermodynamics therefore fixes the \emph{ratio} of the rate
constants, while their \emph{magnitude}, that is, how fast the interface
equilibrates, must come from atomistics or from experiment. This is what
makes the framework a strict generalisation of LTE rather than a competing
model: the same thermodynamic data that parameterise Eqs.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss})
and~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}) parameterise Eq.~(\ref{eq:mass_action}), and the
additional information supplied is exactly the information LTE assumes away.
We stress that Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_general}) is a modelling
constraint on the user rather than something the discretisation can enforce;
Sec.~\ref{sec:params} returns to how the ratios are obtained in practice.

Finally, the framework is agnostic to the nature of the two sides. The
gas/solid dissociation--recombination boundary condition already available in
\FESTIM{} is recovered as the special case in which $A$ is a gas phase, and the
models below differ from one another only in their stoichiometry and in the
number of channels they carry. Figure~\ref{fig:closures} collects them on the
same geometry, together with the LTE closure they generalise.

\begin{figure*}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{interface_models_schematic.pdf}
    \caption{The four interface closures on the same two-subdomain geometry,
    differing only in the chemistry allowed on $\Gamma$. (a) LTE: nothing
    crosses, the two interfacial concentrations being tied by the algebraic
    constraint of Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss}), which gives the interface no rate
    constant, no timescale and no resistance. (b) Model 1,
    Eq.~(\ref{eq:model1_flux}): an atom crosses without changing chemical
    identity, first order in either direction. (c) Model 2,
    Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}): two metal-side atoms recombine into one
    molecular carrier, so the atomic flux leaving the metal is twice the channel
    rate, Eq.~(\ref{eq:atom_balance}). (d) Model 3: the same atom has two exits
    of different order, recombination (quadratic) and fluorination (linear),
    and the branching ratio $\Bra$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:branching}) sets how the
    atomic flux divides between them.}
    \label{fig:closures}
\end{figure*}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Model 1: first-order exchange}
\label{sec:model1}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The simplest case is a single channel $X_A \rightleftharpoons X_B$ in which an
atom crosses the interface without changing chemical identity, as at a
metal/metal interface (Fig.~\ref{fig:closures}b).
Equation~(\ref{eq:mass_action}) then reduces to a
Robin-type flux condition,

\begin{equation}
    \phi = k^{+} c_A\big|_\Gamma - k^{-} c_B\big|_\Gamma ,
    \label{eq:model1_flux}
\end{equation}

with both rate constants an exchange velocity in $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, closed
by mass conservation, $-D_A \,\partial_n c_A = D_B\, \partial_n c_B = \phi$.
Setting $\phi = 0$ and requiring the resulting concentration ratio to coincide
with Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss}) fixes

\begin{equation}
    \frac{k^{+}}{k^{-}} = \frac{K_{S,B}(T)}{K_{S,A}(T)} ,
    \label{eq:detailed_balance_1}
\end{equation}

which is Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_general}) for this channel. Taking
$k^{+}, k^{-} \rightarrow \infty$ at fixed ratio recovers
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss}) exactly, since a finite flux then requires the bracket in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:model1_flux}) to vanish. The derivation is given in
App.~\ref{app:limits}; the correction at finite rate constants is
$\mathcal{O}(\phi/k^{+}c)$ and is quantified in Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Model 2: recombination into a molecular carrier}
\label{sec:model2}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The second case retains a single channel but changes the chemical identity of
the transported species, $2\,X_A \rightleftharpoons Y_{A_2}$: atomic hydrogen
dissolved in the metal recombines into a molecule that dissolves physically in
the liquid (Fig.~\ref{fig:closures}c). It is the metal/molten-salt analogue of
surface recombination, and its net rate is

\begin{equation}
    w_{\mathrm{rec}} = k_{\mathrm{r}}^{+}\left(\cm{\mathrm{H}}\big|_\Gamma\right)^{2}
                     - k_{\mathrm{r}}^{-}\,\cs{\mathrm{H_2}}\big|_\Gamma ,
    \label{eq:model2_rate}
\end{equation}

with interface conditions
$-D^{\mathrm{m}}\partial_n \cm{\mathrm{H}} = 2 w_{\mathrm{rec}}$ and
$D^{\mathrm{s}}\partial_n \cs{\mathrm{H_2}} = w_{\mathrm{rec}}$, the factor two
being the stoichiometry of Eq.~(\ref{eq:atom_balance}). Imposing
$w_{\mathrm{rec}} = 0$ together with Sieverts' law in the metal
($c^{\mathrm{m}} = K_S\sqrt{P}$) and Henry's law in the liquid
($c^{\mathrm{s}} = K_H P$) gives

\begin{equation}
    \frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}^{+}}{k_{\mathrm{r}}^{-}}
    = \frac{K_{H}(T)}{K_{S}(T)^{2}} .
    \label{eq:detailed_balance_2}
\end{equation}

Substituting Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_2}) back into
$w_{\mathrm{rec}} = 0$ returns precisely the Sieverts/Henry condition,
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}). The mixed-law interface currently available in
\FESTIM{}~v2 \cite{dark_festim_2026} is therefore the fast-kinetics limit of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}). Stated plainly: the two interface laws of
Sec.~\ref{sec:lte} are not two separate physical models to be selected between,
but the fast limits of two different channels of the same framework. Which one
applies is a question about chemistry, not about which option to set in an
input file.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Model 3: competing channels}
\label{sec:model3}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Nothing in Sec.~\ref{sec:general} restricts the interface to a single channel,
and at a metal/fluoride-melt interface there is no reason to expect one.
Hydrogen leaving the metal may recombine and dissolve molecularly, or it may be
oxidised by the melt and dissolve as HF, which is far more soluble, corrosive,
and redox dependent. We therefore let two channels operate on the same
interface (Fig.~\ref{fig:closures}d),
\begin{align*}
    \text{R:}&\quad 2\,\mathrm{H(m)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H_2(s)} \\
    \text{F:}&\quad \mathrm{H(m)} + \mathrm{F^-(s)} + h^{+}
                    \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{HF(s)} ,
\end{align*}
where $h^{+}$ denotes the oxidising half of the salt redox couple. In practice
the fluorine potential is set by a buffer, either beryllium metal or an
imposed $\mathrm{HF/H_2}$ ratio in the cover gas, so we write channel F with an
effective activity $a_\mathrm{F}$ lumping the fluoride activity and the redox
potential,

\begin{equation}
    w_{\mathrm{F}} = k_{\mathrm{f}}^{+}\, a_{\mathrm{F}}\,
                     \cm{\mathrm{H}}\big|_\Gamma
                   - k_{\mathrm{f}}^{-}\,\cs{\mathrm{HF}}\big|_\Gamma ,
    \label{eq:model3_rate}
\end{equation}

while $w_\mathrm{rec}$ retains the form of Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}). The
atomic flux leaving the metal is now shared between the two,

\begin{equation}
    -D^{\mathrm{m}}\nabla \cm{\mathrm{H}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\big|_\Gamma
    = 2\,w_{\mathrm{rec}} + w_{\mathrm{F}} ,
    \label{eq:model3_total_flux}
\end{equation}

and each channel feeds its own carrier in the salt through
Eq.~(\ref{eq:bc_salt}).

The two channels are of different order in the interfacial loading: R is
quadratic in $\cm{\mathrm{H}}$ and F is linear. Their competition is measured
by the branching ratio

\begin{equation}
    \Bra
    \equiv \frac{w_{\mathrm{F}}}{2\,w_{\mathrm{rec}}}
    \;\xrightarrow[\text{far from equilibrium}]{}\;
    \frac{k_{\mathrm{f}}^{+} a_{\mathrm{F}}}
         {2\,k_{\mathrm{r}}^{+}\,\cm{\mathrm{H}}\big|_\Gamma} ,
    \label{eq:branching}
\end{equation}

whose consequences form the core of the argument of this paper.

First, $\Bra$ depends \emph{inversely} on the interfacial loading. At low
loading the fluorination channel dominates and the salt-side inventory scales
linearly with $\cm{\mathrm{H}}$; at high loading recombination takes over and
the scaling becomes quadratic. Defining the apparent interfacial exponent

\begin{equation}
    n \equiv \frac{\partial \ln \left(\text{total salt-side H}\right)}
                  {\partial \ln \cm{\mathrm{H}}} ,
    \qquad 1 \le n \le 2 ,
    \label{eq:apparent_exponent}
\end{equation}

LTE requires $n$ to be a constant fixed in advance: $n = 2$ for the
Sieverts/Henry closure of Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}), $n = 1$ for a Henry/Henry-like
closure. Here $n$ is instead a solution-dependent quantity that drifts during a
transient as the interfacial loading builds up. No fixed algebraic interface
law can reproduce this behaviour.

Second, $\Bra$ depends on the salt redox state through $a_\mathrm{F}$, which is
itself dynamic: HF production, corrosion of the container and depletion of the
buffer all move it. The behaviour of the interface is therefore coupled to the
chemistry of the salt, and is not a material property of the metal/salt pair
that could be tabulated once and reused.

Third, fast kinetics does not rescue LTE here. Even when
both channels are individually equilibrated, with a large Damk\"ohler number
for each in the sense of Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler}, the \emph{partition} of the
flux between them is set by the ratio of the forward rate constants and not by
thermodynamics. Making each channel faster makes each channel individually
closer to its own equilibrium while leaving the split between them kinetic.

One objection to all of this is worth answering in full. It is possible in
principle to close the interface without any kinetic information, by imposing
full local chemical equilibrium among all interfacial species, a Gibbs
minimisation at $\Gamma$ at the prescribed fluorine potential. That closure is, however, (i) not what any macroscopic
hydrogen transport code implements; (ii) valid only if every interconversion is
fast compared with transport, which is exactly what the available evidence puts
in doubt \cite{hodille_molecular_2022,calderoni_measurement_2008}; and
(iii) still silent on how a given net flux divides between carriers, since
equilibrium fixes concentrations and not fluxes. The last point is the decisive
one for the quantity measured in a permeation experiment.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Multiple isotopes: isotopologue channels}
\label{sec:isotopes}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The framework extends to $N_\mathrm{iso}$ mobile atomic species in the metal
without modification, but the structure that emerges is qualitatively new.
Recombination now populates every isotopologue and fluorination every fluoride.
For $\{\mathrm{H,T}\}$ the recombination channels are

\begin{align}
    w_{\mathrm{HH}} &= k_{\mathrm{HH}}^{+}\left(\cm{\mathrm{H}}\right)^{2}
                     - k_{\mathrm{HH}}^{-}\,\cs{\mathrm{H_2}}
    \label{eq:iso_HH}\\
    w_{\mathrm{HT}} &= k_{\mathrm{HT}}^{+}\,\cm{\mathrm{H}}\,\cm{\mathrm{T}}
                     - k_{\mathrm{HT}}^{-}\,\cs{\mathrm{HT}}
    \label{eq:iso_HT}\\
    w_{\mathrm{TT}} &= k_{\mathrm{TT}}^{+}\left(\cm{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{2}
                     - k_{\mathrm{TT}}^{-}\,\cs{\mathrm{T_2}}
    \label{eq:iso_TT}
\end{align}

and the fluorination channels are

\begin{align}
    w_{\mathrm{HF}} &= k_{\mathrm{f,H}}^{+} a_{\mathrm{F}} \cm{\mathrm{H}}
                     - k_{\mathrm{f,H}}^{-}\,\cs{\mathrm{HF}}
    \label{eq:iso_HF}\\
    w_{\mathrm{TF}} &= k_{\mathrm{f,T}}^{+} a_{\mathrm{F}} \cm{\mathrm{T}}
                     - k_{\mathrm{f,T}}^{-}\,\cs{\mathrm{TF}} .
    \label{eq:iso_TF}
\end{align}

The metal-side conditions follow from Eq.~(\ref{eq:bc_metal}) with the
stoichiometry of Eq.~(\ref{eq:atom_balance}),

\begin{align}
    -D^{\mathrm{m}}_\mathrm{H}\nabla \cm{\mathrm{H}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\big|_\Gamma
        &= 2\,w_{\mathrm{HH}} + w_{\mathrm{HT}} + w_{\mathrm{HF}}
    \label{eq:iso_flux_H}\\
    -D^{\mathrm{m}}_\mathrm{T}\nabla \cm{\mathrm{T}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\big|_\Gamma
        &= 2\,w_{\mathrm{TT}} + w_{\mathrm{HT}} + w_{\mathrm{TF}} ,
    \label{eq:iso_flux_T}
\end{align}

each salt-side carrier being fed by the single channel that produces it.

All five channels are written with the plain mass-action form of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:mass_action}), with no combinatorial prefactor. This is the
convention used throughout \FESTIM{} for volumetric reactions, gas/solid
surface reactions and interfacial channels alike: the rate is the product of
the reactant concentrations, and stoichiometry enters only through the
coefficients $\nu_{ir}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:bc_metal}), that is, through how many
times a channel is counted in the flux of a given species.

The statistical degeneracy of the mixed pair is therefore a statement about the
\emph{value} of $k_{\mathrm{HT}}^{+}$, not an extra factor in the rate law.
Counting encounters makes this concrete. If $\kappa$ is the rate constant per
encounter of two interfacial atoms, the number of distinct identical pairs
available per unit area is $\tfrac{1}{2}(\cm{\mathrm{H}})^2$, whereas an H and
a T are distinguishable and give $\cm{\mathrm{H}}\cm{\mathrm{T}}$ distinct
pairs. Writing the homonuclear channels as
$k_{\mathrm{HH}}^{+}(\cm{\mathrm{H}})^2$ absorbs the one-half into
$k_{\mathrm{HH}}^{+}$, so the same per-encounter constant corresponds to

\begin{equation}
    k_{\mathrm{HT}}^{+} = 2\,k_{\mathrm{HH}}^{+} = 2\,k_{\mathrm{TT}}^{+}
    \qquad \text{(mass-independent limit)} ,
    \label{eq:stat_factor}
\end{equation}

with the reverse constants equal. Setting
Eqs.~(\ref{eq:iso_HH})--(\ref{eq:iso_TT}) to zero under
Eq.~(\ref{eq:stat_factor}) then gives
$\cs{\mathrm{H_2}} = (k_{\mathrm{HH}}^{+}/k^{-})(\cm{\mathrm{H}})^2$,
$\cs{\mathrm{T_2}} = (k_{\mathrm{HH}}^{+}/k^{-})(\cm{\mathrm{T}})^2$ and
$\cs{\mathrm{HT}} = 2(k_{\mathrm{HH}}^{+}/k^{-})\cm{\mathrm{H}}\cm{\mathrm{T}}$,
so that

\begin{equation}
    K_\mathrm{exch}
    = \frac{\cs{\mathrm{HT}}^{2}}{\cs{\mathrm{H_2}}\,\cs{\mathrm{T_2}}}
    = 4 .
    \label{eq:kexch}
\end{equation}

Four is the classical value for
$\mathrm{H_2} + \mathrm{T_2} \rightleftharpoons 2\,\mathrm{HT}$, obtained
from the symmetry numbers $\sigma_{\mathrm{H_2}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{T_2}} = 2$
and $\sigma_{\mathrm{HT}} = 1$, and it states that random pairing of equal H
and T populations yields $\mathrm{H_2 : HT : T_2} = 1 : 2 : 1$. Taking all
three forward constants equal instead, which is the natural reading of
Eqs.~(\ref{eq:iso_HH})--(\ref{eq:iso_TT}) if Eq.~(\ref{eq:stat_factor}) is
overlooked, yields $K_\mathrm{exch} = 1$ and an equal split, which would
suppress the scrambling discussed below. Both cases are reproduced to solver
tolerance by the zero-flux test of App.~\ref{app:conservation}. Any departure
from $K_\mathrm{exch} = 4$ is a genuine isotope effect and belongs in the rate
constants themselves; the zero-point-energy corrections are collected in
App.~\ref{app:balance}.

The counting is what makes the multi-isotope case structurally different. Two
mobile species on the metal side feed five carriers on the salt side,

\begin{equation}
    N_\mathrm{iso} \;\longrightarrow\;
    \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}N_\mathrm{iso}(N_\mathrm{iso}+1)}_{\text{molecular}}
    + \underbrace{N_\mathrm{iso}}_{\text{fluoride}},
    \qquad
    \mathrm{(H,T)}:\; 2 \rightarrow 3 + 2 = 5 ,
    \label{eq:counting}
\end{equation}

whereas LTE supplies one scalar constraint per transported species pair. With
two metal-side and five salt-side interfacial unknowns the per-species
Dirichlet closure is underdetermined. It is also wrong in form, which is the
more serious objection: by Eq.~(\ref{eq:iso_HT}) the HT concentration is set by
the \emph{product} $\cm{\mathrm{H}}\cm{\mathrm{T}}$, so the
metal~$\rightarrow$~salt map is bilinear and non-diagonal, and a condition of
the form $c_i/K_{S,i} = f(c_i')$ cannot represent an off-diagonal coupling. In
the multi-isotope case LTE is not inaccurate; it is ill-posed.

Isotope exchange, $\mathrm{H_2} + \mathrm{T_2} \rightleftharpoons
2\,\mathrm{HT}$, is slow in the homogeneous phase but is catalysed by metal
surfaces. In the present framework it requires no additional reaction: it
emerges from Eqs.~(\ref{eq:iso_HH})--(\ref{eq:iso_TT}), since a $\mathrm{T_2}$
molecule may dissociate at $\Gamma$ and deposit T into the lattice, and lattice
T may subsequently leave paired with an H. Interfacial scrambling is thus a
prediction of the model rather than an input to it.

Two observable consequences follow, neither of which is expressible under LTE.
The first is \emph{isotope swamping}: the tritium flux depends on the protium
inventory, because raising $\cm{\mathrm{H}}$ shifts tritium from the
$\mathrm{T_2}$ channel to the statistically favoured HT channel and thereby
changes the total tritium throughput. A diagonal closure predicts no such
coupling. The second is \emph{interfacial fractionation}: because the
recombination and fluorination channels carry different isotope dependences
($k_\mathrm{HH}$ against $k_\mathrm{HT}$ against $k_\mathrm{TT}$;
$k_\mathrm{f,H}$ against $k_\mathrm{f,T}$), the isotopic composition of the
permeating flux differs from that of the metal, and differs between the
molecular and the fluoride carrier. We report it as the model output
$\alpha = (\mathrm{T/H})_\mathrm{flux} / (\mathrm{T/H})_\mathrm{metal}$.

Detailed balance applies channel by channel as in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_general}), the ratios $k^{+}/k^{-}$ being
constrained by the isotopologue equilibrium constants. These are close to, but
not exactly, the classical statistical values, the departure arising from
zero-point energy; they are collected in App.~\ref{app:balance}.

\subsection{Dimensionless criteria for LTE validity}
\label{sec:damkohler}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The framework of Sec.~\ref{sec:general} contains LTE as a limit, so it can also
say when that limit is legitimate. Three questions have to be answered, in
increasing order of structural severity.

\paragraph{Within a channel} The relevant comparison is between interfacial
equilibration and bulk transport. Because channels may be of different order,
the comparison is made with the exchange velocity obtained by linearising the
channel about the interfacial state,
$k_r^\mathrm{eff} \equiv \partial w_r / \partial \cm{}\big|_\Gamma$, which
reduces to $k^{+}$ for the first-order Model 1 and to
$2 k_\mathrm{r}^{+} \cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma$ for the recombination channel of
Model 2. The Damk\"ohler number of channel $r$ is then

\begin{equation}
    \Da_r = \frac{k_r^\mathrm{eff}\,L}{D} .
    \label{eq:damkohler}
\end{equation}

When $\Da_r \gg 1$ the channel is locally equilibrated and its algebraic LTE
form is recovered, as shown in Secs.~\ref{sec:model1} and~\ref{sec:model2}. For
asymmetric systems one Damk\"ohler number should be evaluated per side, using
$D_A, L_A$ and $D_B, L_B$ respectively, and the smaller of the two governs.

For a channel of order one, as in Model 1, $k^\mathrm{eff}$ is a constant and
$\Da$ is an input to the problem. For the recombination channel of Model 2 it
is neither. The two sides linearise differently: the metal side sees an
exchange velocity $2k_\mathrm{r}^{+}\cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma$, which falls as
the interface is depleted, while the salt side sees $k_\mathrm{r}^{-}$, which
is constant because the reverse step is first order in $\cs{\mathrm{H_2}}$. The
interfacial resistance $(2k_\mathrm{r}^{+}\cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma)^{-1}$ thus
grows as the loading falls, and the same interface may be effectively
equilibrated at high loading while being kinetically limited early in a
permeation transient, before the upstream side has charged. Two consequences
follow for what comes later. The additive interfacial resistance of
Sec.~\ref{sec:analytical} is exact only for Model 1, and for Model 2 holds
about an operating point rather than as a constant in series with the bulk.
And a parametric sweep in $\Da$ requires a reference loading to be fixed: we
use the upstream Sieverts value $c^{\star} = K_S\sqrt{P_\mathrm{up}}$, giving
the control parameter $2k_\mathrm{r}^{+}c^{\star}L/D$, and report the local
value attained at the interface as a diagnostic.

The caveat carried over from Ref.~\cite{silva-solis_hydrogen_2026} applies
throughout: $\Da \gg 1$ is \emph{necessary but not sufficient}, since under a
net flux the steady state departs from equilibrium by a term proportional to
$\phi/k^{+}$, which LTE sets to zero by construction.

\paragraph{Between channels} The relevant group is the branching ratio $\Bra$
of Eq.~(\ref{eq:branching}). In the limits $\Bra \rightarrow 0$ and
$\Bra \rightarrow \infty$ one channel dominates and an LTE form exists,
Sieverts/Henry and linear respectively. When $\Bra = \mathcal{O}(1)$, no
single algebraic law applies at all, however large the Damk\"ohler numbers are.

The two groups are independent, and Fig.~\ref{fig:regime_map} puts them on the
two axes of a single map. The field plotted is the larger of two quantities,

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E} = \max\left(
        \underbrace{\frac{1}{1 + \Da}}_{\text{within a channel}} ,\;
        \underbrace{\frac{\min(1, \Bra)}{1 + \Bra}}_{\text{between channels}}
    \right) ,
    \label{eq:lte_indicator}
\end{equation}

the first being the relative error made on the flux by treating a channel as
equilibrated, which is exact for Model 1 by Eq.~(\ref{eq:model1_convergence}),
and the second the fraction of the atomic flux carried by the minority channel,
which is the part of the flux that no single-channel closure represents at all.
Taking the larger of the two is a convention: $\mathcal{E}$ is an indicator of
how badly the best available LTE closure does, not a bound on the error of any
particular simulation. The map is nevertheless entirely analytical, and the
right-hand axis carries the apparent exponent
$n = (2+\Bra)/(1+\Bra)$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:n_of_B}), which is what a permeation
experiment would report.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{regime_map.pdf}
    \caption{Where a local-equilibrium interface is defensible. The indicator
    $\mathcal{E}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_indicator}) over the $(\Da, \Bra)$ plane,
    with contours at $1\%$ and $10\%$. The abscissa is the two-sided group
    $\Da^{\star}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:defect_1_explicit}), the ratio of the total
    bulk resistance to the interfacial resistance $1/k^{+}$, since that is the
    combination the flux error depends on.
    LTE requires both a fast channel and a
    dominant one: the pale regions at bottom right and top right are the
    Sieverts/Henry and linear closures, the left-hand strip is kinetically
    limited whatever the branching, and the band around $\Bra = 1$ admits no
    algebraic interface law however fast the kinetics. The right-hand axis gives
    the apparent exponent $n$ corresponding to each branching ratio.}
    \label{fig:regime_map}
\end{figure}

Read as a decision aid, the map says that an LTE interface needs $\Da \gtrsim
10$ \emph{and} a branching ratio at least an order of magnitude away from unity.
Neither condition alone suffices, and the second is the one that the literature
does not check.

\paragraph{Across isotopes} LTE further requires the metal~$\rightarrow$~salt
map to be diagonal. The departure is quantified by the fraction of the tritium
flux carried by the mixed isotopologue,

\begin{equation}
    \chi_\mathrm{HT} = \frac{w_\mathrm{HT}}{w_\mathrm{HT} + 2 w_\mathrm{TT}} .
    \label{eq:chi_HT}
\end{equation}

A diagonal, per-isotope description is recovered as
$\chi_\mathrm{HT} \rightarrow 0$ and is forbidden when
$\chi_\mathrm{HT} = \mathcal{O}(1)$. In the tritium-lean, protium-rich
conditions typical of a breeding blanket, $\chi_\mathrm{HT} \rightarrow 1$.

\section{Implementation in \FESTIM}
\label{sec:implementation}
% ===============================================================

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Weak formulation}
\label{sec:weak}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Each subdomain is discretised on its own submesh of a common parent mesh and
carries its own function space for every species it hosts, so that the
concentration fields are free to jump across $\Gamma$. This is the multi-species
discontinuous architecture of \FESTIM{}~v2 \cite{dark_festim_2026}, built on
submeshes and entity maps in DOLFINx \cite{baratta_dolfinx_2023}. Multiplying the transport equation of species $i$
on $\Omega_A$ by a test function $v \in H^1(\Omega_A)$ and integrating the
diffusive term by parts gives the residual

\begin{equation}
    F^{\mathrm{m}}_i =
    \int_{\Omega_A}\frac{\partial \cm{i}}{\partial t}\, v \,\mathrm{d}V
    + \int_{\Omega_A} D^{\mathrm{m}}_i \nabla \cm{i}\cdot\nabla v \,\mathrm{d}V
    - \int_{\partial\Omega_A} D^{\mathrm{m}}_i
      \nabla \cm{i}\cdot\mathbf{n}\; v \,\mathrm{d}S ,
    \label{eq:weak_bulk}
\end{equation}

and the interface enters only through the part of the boundary integral carried
by $\Gamma$. Substituting Eqs.~(\ref{eq:bc_metal}) and~(\ref{eq:bc_salt}) leaves
the surface contributions

\begin{align}
    F^{\mathrm{m}}_i &\supset
    \phantom{-}\int_\Gamma \Big(\sum_r \nu_{ir}\, w_r\Big)\,
        v^{\mathrm{m}}_i \,\mathrm{d}S
    \label{eq:weak_metal}\\
    F^{\mathrm{s}}_\alpha &\supset
    -\int_\Gamma \Big(\sum_r \mu_{\alpha r}\, w_r\Big)\,
        v^{\mathrm{s}}_\alpha \,\mathrm{d}S ,
    \label{eq:weak_salt}
\end{align}

with $w_r$ given by Eq.~(\ref{eq:mass_action}) and evaluated on the traces of
the solution from either side. The opposite signs are those of the two outward
normals: a channel proceeding forwards removes atoms from $\Omega_A$ and
delivers carriers to $\Omega_B$. Because $\Gamma$ is an interior facet of the
parent mesh, the integrals in Eqs.~(\ref{eq:weak_metal})
and~(\ref{eq:weak_salt}) are assembled with an interior-facet measure whose
integration data map each facet to the cell it belongs to on each side, one
restriction per subdomain.

The stoichiometric coefficients $\nu_{ir}$ and $\mu_{\alpha r}$ enter as
multiplicities rather than as numerical prefactors: the rate $w_r$ is added once
to the residual of each occurrence of a species in the channel. Declaring the
recombination channel of Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}) with two H reactants
therefore produces both the square in the rate law and the factor two in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:bc_metal}), and nothing else has to be specified. One consequence
deserves stating, because the natural guess is wrong. The statistical degeneracy
of the mixed isotopologue, Eq.~(\ref{eq:stat_factor}), cannot be encoded by
declaring the HT channel twice with the reactants in either order: $w_r$ carries
both directions, so duplicating the channel doubles the forward and the reverse
term equally, rescaling the channel in time while leaving $K_\mathrm{exch}$
unchanged at unity. The degeneracy is a forward-only count and belongs in the
value of $k^{+}_\mathrm{HT}$.

\KEY{No interfacial degrees of freedom are introduced.} Every channel is
algebraic in the trace values, so no function space on $\Gamma$ and no
additional unknowns are required, only the coupling of the two submeshes through
entity maps. This is the simplification relative to the companion codimension-1
work \cite{festim_codim1_inprep}, where an interfacial inventory is transported
along $\Gamma$ and does carry its own degrees of freedom.

The assembled system is blocked, with one residual block per subdomain, and the
surface integrals of Eqs.~(\ref{eq:weak_metal}) and~(\ref{eq:weak_salt}) are the
only terms coupling one block to another. The Jacobian is obtained by symbolic
differentiation of every block with respect to every unknown and is therefore
exact; the off-diagonal blocks are populated exactly where a channel connects a
metal-side species to a salt-side one. For Model 1 those blocks are constant,
for the recombination channel they are linear in the interfacial concentration,
and for the isotopologue channels of Sec.~\ref{sec:isotopes} the block coupling
$\cm{\mathrm{H}}$ to $\cs{\mathrm{HT}}$ carries $\cm{\mathrm{T}}$ and vice
versa, which is the bilinear structure of Eq.~(\ref{eq:iso_HT}) appearing in the
linear algebra. Several channels sharing one interface are declared as separate
objects and their contributions add, which is how Model 3 and the five-channel
isotopologue set of Sec.~\ref{sec:isotopes} are assembled. The blocks are
collected in App.~\ref{app:weak}.

\subsection{Numerical considerations}
\label{sec:numerics}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Four aspects of the discretisation deserve comment. We state where each of them
currently stands rather than claiming the open ones are settled.

The first is stiffness at large Damk\"ohler number. As $k^{+}$ grows at fixed
ratio, the surface terms of Eqs.~(\ref{eq:weak_metal})
and~(\ref{eq:weak_salt}) come to dominate the bulk terms of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:weak_bulk}), which scale as $D/L$, and the interface condition
degenerates towards the algebraic constraint of Sec.~\ref{sec:lte}. No scaling
of the interface residual is applied at present. For the first-order channel of
Model 1 this turns out not to matter: the steady problem is linear, Newton
converges in one iteration at every point of the sweep of
Sec.~\ref{sec:lte_limit}, and the only trace of the growing stiffness is a loss
of about one significant figure in the interfacial concentrations between
$\Da = 10^{2}$ and $\Da = 10^{6}$, from a relative agreement of $10^{-13}$ with
the closed form to $5\times10^{-12}$.
\VERIFY{whether the higher-order channels behave as well; the corresponding
sweep for Model 2 has not yet been run, and it is there that a residual scaling
may become necessary.}

The second is positivity. The channels of order two are quadratic and bilinear
in the trace concentrations, and nothing in the formulation prevents a solution
from undershooting to negative values near a steep front. A negative excursion
in $\cm{\mathrm{H}}$ leaves the recombination rate of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}) positive, since the concentration enters squared, so
the interface goes on producing carrier where it should be inert; the bilinear
channel of Eq.~(\ref{eq:iso_HT}) instead changes sign. No positivity safeguard
is implemented, and we control the problem by refining the mesh and the time
step near the initial transient and by monitoring the minimum trace value. This
is a genuine open item rather than an omission from the description.

The third is the recovery of LTE, which requires no special-casing. The same
interface objects are used at every Damk\"ohler number, and the LTE solution is
approached continuously as $k^{\pm} \rightarrow \infty$ at fixed ratio; there is
no switch to a different interface class and no reformulation of the problem.
Sec.~\ref{sec:verification} demonstrates this numerically.

The fourth is cost. Each channel adds one facet assembly over $\Gamma$ and
populates the Jacobian blocks it couples. Since no interfacial unknowns are
introduced, the size of the linear system is unchanged relative to the LTE
interface on the same mesh, and the added work scales with the number of facets
on $\Gamma$ rather than with the number of volume degrees of freedom.

\subsection{Parameterisation}
\label{sec:params}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Each channel needs two numbers, and they are of different kinds. Detailed
balance, Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_general}), constrains the \emph{ratio}
$k_r^{+}/k_r^{-}$ from the same thermodynamic data that parameterise the LTE
conditions of Sec.~\ref{sec:lte}, so no new information is needed there. The
\emph{magnitude} is the additional input the framework requires, and it can come
from atomistics where a suitable calculation exists, from fitting to permeation
breakthrough transients, or, as a first estimate for the recombination channel,
from the gas/metal recombination coefficients measured for the same metal. The
sensitivity of a prediction to that magnitude is exactly what the Damk\"ohler
number of Eq.~(\ref{eq:damkohler}) measures: where $\Da \gg 1$ an order of
magnitude in $k^{+}$ is immaterial, and where it is not, the interface is
rate-limiting and the number has to be justified.

Two properties of the present implementation should be read as limitations of
the code rather than of the framework. The rate constants are prescribed as
plain numbers, with no Arrhenius form, so a temperature dependence has to be
imposed by the user, and the activity $a_\mathrm{F}$ of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:model3_rate}) is folded into $k_\mathrm{f}^{+}$ by hand. More
importantly, the ratio $k_r^{+}/k_r^{-}$ is not checked against
Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_general}) by the solver: thermodynamic consistency
is a property of how the model is parameterised, and it remains the
responsibility of whoever writes the input. Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations} returns
to both points.

For the fluorination channel, $a_\mathrm{F}$ is tied to the redox state of the
salt and is therefore an operating condition rather than a material constant.

The transport data available for FLiBe do not make any of this easy. Reported
solubilities and diffusivities for $\mathrm{H_2}$ and HF, and a fortiori for
their isotopologues, span orders of magnitude \cite{libra_2022}. That spread is
the reason an interface model whose parameters can be \emph{inferred} from
permeation data is useful, rather than an argument against attempting one.

Part of the spread, however, is not experimental scatter. As developed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:units}, a tabulated solubility carries units of
$\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}\,Pa^{-1}}$ or $\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}\,Pa^{-1/2}}$ according
to whether Henry's or Sieverts' law was assumed in reducing the measurement, and
the two are not interconvertible by any fixed factor. A literature value
therefore cannot be used without knowing which carrier was assumed when it was
obtained. Whenever a solubility is quoted in this paper, the assumed law is
quoted with it.

\subsection{Recovery of LTE in the fast-kinetics limit}
\label{sec:lte_limit}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The first test is Model 1 on a one-dimensional two-slab problem, with
$D_A = 0.5$ and $D_B = 1$ in arbitrary units, slabs of equal thickness $0.5$,
concentrations $c_0 = 2$ and $c_L = 1$ imposed at the outer faces, and
$k^{-}/k^{+} = 0.5$. The steady problem is solved for seventeen values of the
Damk\"ohler number spanning eight decades, $\Da = k^{+}L_A/D_A$ from $10^{-2}$
to $10^{6}$, and each solution is compared both with the closed form of
App.~\ref{app:analytical} and with the LTE condition
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss}) that the channel should approach.
Figure~\ref{fig:two_slab} shows the problem and the two readings of its steady
solution that the rest of this section uses. Plotting the two slabs on a common
scale, $c_A$ on one side and $c_B/K$ on the other, turns Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss})
into continuity, so what is left at $\Gamma$ is the defect
$\Delta = \phi/k^{+}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:defect_1_explicit}): it closes as
$\Da$ grows and is nonzero at any finite rate constant, because the interface is
carrying a flux. The transient behaviour
over the same sweep is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:lte_transient}, and makes the
first of the three consequences listed in Sec.~\ref{sec:lte} concrete: at
moderate $\Da$ the interfacial ratio is far from its equilibrium value for most
of the transient, which is the timescale an algebraic closure denies the
interface.

interfacial concentrations agree with

$6\times10^{-12}$ over the whole sweep, which is round-off: the
piecewise linear steady profile is represented exactly by the P1 elements, so
this is a check of the interface term itself and not of the bulk discretisation.
The departure from LTE, in the lower panel, is monotone and
first order in $1/\Da$. The order measured between successive points reaches
$0.997$ at $\Da = 10^{2}$ and $1.0000$ above $\Da = 10^{4}$, and the departure
itself falls from $1.5\times10^{-2}$ at $\Da = 10^{2}$ to $1.5\times10^{-6}$ at
$\Da = 10^{6}$, approaching the asymptote $1.5/\Da$ predicted by
Eq.~(\ref{eq:defect_1_explicit}) for these parameters. The rate of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:model1_convergence}) is therefore confirmed, and with it the claim
that LTE is recovered continuously rather than imposed.

The second test replaces the channel with the recombination channel of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}) on a metal/liquid pair, which is the case this paper
is about. Geometry, diffusivities and outer concentrations are those of the
Model 1 test, so that only the interface changes: the slab $[0, 0.5]$ is the
metal and carries the atomic species $\mathrm{H}$, the slab $[0.5, 1]$ is the
liquid and carries the molecular carrier $\mathrm{H_2}$, and the two are now
distinct species rather than one species restricted to two subdomains. The
channel is declared with two reactants and one product, so that the
stoichiometric factor two of Eq.~(\ref{eq:bc_metal}) follows from the
declaration rather than being imposed by hand, and detailed balance is applied
through Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_2}) with $K_H/K_S^{2} = 0.5$.

Since the channel is second order, $\Da$ is not an input to the problem. We
follow Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler} and sweep the control parameter
$2k_\mathrm{r}^{+}c_0L_\mathrm{m}/D_\mathrm{m}$ over the same eight decades,
reporting the value $2k_\mathrm{r}^{+}\cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma
L_\mathrm{m}/D_\mathrm{m}$ attained at the interface as a diagnostic. The
reference solution is the closed form Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_analytical}), which is
the root of a quadratic rather than a sum of resistances in series.

interfacial concentrations agree with Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_analytical}) to a
relative error of at most $2.2\times10^{-11}$, and to below $10^{-14}$ at all
but two points of the sweep, so the nonlinear interface term is solved to the
accuracy of the Newton solve and not merely to that of the discretisation. The
interfacial concentrations approach the Sieverts/Henry values
$\cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma = 3/2$ and $\cs{\mathrm{H_2}}|_\Gamma = 9/8$ to within
a relative $1.9\times10^{-7}$ at $\Da = 10^{6}$, and the departure from
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}) falls from $4.4\times10^{-3}$ at $\Da = 10^{2}$ to
$4.4\times10^{-7}$ at $\Da = 10^{6}$. The order measured between successive
points is $0.994$ at $\Da = 10^{2}$ and $0.9999$ or better above
$\Da = 10^{4}$, and the departure approaches the asymptote $0.444/\Da$ that
Eq.~(\ref{eq:defect_2})
predicts for these parameters. Convergence to LTE is therefore first order in
$1/\Da$ for the second-order channel as well, on the quantity the channel
equilibrates rather than on the concentration ratio.

Two features of the sweep are specific to the higher-order channel. The
Damk\"ohler number attained at the interface, which the sweep reports as a
diagnostic, settles at $3/4$ of the control value, the ratio
$\cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma/c_0$ of the equilibrated interfacial loading to the
loading the control parameter is built from; at small $\Da$ the interface is
barely depleted and the two coincide. By
Eq.~(\ref{eq:two_slab_traces_2}) that ratio is
$1 - 2w_\mathrm{rec}R_\mathrm{m}/c_0$, the fraction of the upstream loading
left once the metal-side bulk resistance has taken its share of the drop, so
the offset is fixed by the bulk problem and the local Damk\"ohler number can
fall arbitrarily far below the control one in a thick metal. The second
feature is that the additive decomposition of Sec.~\ref{sec:analytical} does
not survive: $1/k_\mathrm{r}^{+}$ is not a resistance in series with the bulk
ones, and it is only about the operating point that
$(2k_\mathrm{r}^{+}\cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma)^{-1}$ plays that role.

\subsection{Analytical steady-state solutions}
\label{sec:analytical}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Three closed-form results underpin the tests above and the regime map of
Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler}; all are derived in App.~\ref{app:analytical}.

For Model 1 on the two-slab problem, combining flux continuity with
Eq.~(\ref{eq:model1_flux}) gives the interfacial concentrations and the
permeating flux in closed form, Eq.~(\ref{eq:model1_analytical}). The flux is
the difference of the outer concentrations divided by a sum of three resistances
in series, the two bulk resistances $L_A/D_A$ and $L_B/(K D_B)$ and an
interfacial resistance $1/k^{+}$ contributed by the channel, so that LTE is the
zero-interfacial-resistance limit and $\Da$ is the ratio of the bulk resistance
to the interfacial one, as drawn in Fig.~\ref{fig:two_slab}b. This is the
solution used in Sec.~\ref{sec:lte_limit}.

Model 2 on the same geometry is still solvable in closed form, but the answer is
the root of a quadratic in the channel rate,
Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_analytical}), rather than a ratio. The three resistances no
longer add, which is the analytical statement of the loading dependence
described in Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler}. This is the solution the metal/liquid
sweep of Sec.~\ref{sec:lte_limit} is checked against.

For Model 3 at steady state, solving the two-channel balance gives the apparent
interfacial exponent of Eq.~(\ref{eq:apparent_exponent}) as a function of the
branching ratio alone, $n = (2 + \Bra)/(1 + \Bra)$, Eq.~(\ref{eq:n_of_B}). It
interpolates continuously between the two exponents that LTE offers as
alternatives, passing through $n = 3/2$ when the two channels carry equal atomic
fluxes, and it is the analytical backbone of the regime-map figure and of the
exponent measurement proposed in Sec.~\ref{sec:hyperion_branching}.

\subsection{A solubility constant has no law-independent units}
\label{sec:units}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}
    \item \TODO{pin the exact location --- which table or equation of
          \cite{calderoni_measurement_2008} carries the mismatched units, and
          whether the inconsistency is between the stated units and the fitted
          pressure exponent, or between the units and the permeability
          $\Phi = D K$ derived from them. Write this paragraph so a referee can
          check it in one minute; a vague accusation will be rejected.}

    \item The argument, stated as a general point rather than as a complaint
          about one paper:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Henry: $c = K_H P
                    \Rightarrow [K_H] = \mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}\,Pa^{-1}}$
              \item Sieverts: $c = K_S\sqrt{P}
                    \Rightarrow [K_S] = \mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}\,Pa^{-1/2}}$
          \end{itemize}
          The \emph{dimensions} of the reported constant encode the assumed
          dissolution law. There is no conversion factor between the two:
          $K_H = K_S^2/c$ holds only along a single isobar, so any
          ``conversion'' silently fixes a reference pressure and is wrong
          everywhere else. A tabulated solubility is therefore not a material
          property that can be quoted independently of a speciation assumption
          --- it is a property of the (material $+$ assumed carrier) pair.

    \item Why this matters here specifically. Calderoni et al.\ report a
          square-root dependence of the permeating flux on pressure for FLiBe
          \cite{calderoni_measurement_2008}, whereas Fukada et al.\ report a
          linear dependence for FLiNaK, and the review literature states the
          expectation that dissolved diatomic $\mathrm{H_2}$ in a molten salt
          should obey Henry's law \cite{forsberg_flibe_2019}.
          \VERIFY{both against the primary sources before writing; the contrast
          is currently taken from a secondary summary \cite{mit_thesis_flibe} and
          needs the Fukada reference proper.}

    \item \KEY{A square-root dependence in an ionic melt is not a measurement
          artefact.} It is the signature of \emph{dissociative} dissolution ---
          i.e.\ of exactly the fluoride-bound / atomic carrier that the same
          authors could not rule out. In the language of Sec.~\ref{sec:model3}, a
          system running with $\Bra \gg 1$ (fluorination-dominated) presents an
          apparent exponent $n \rightarrow 1$, i.e.\ a Sieverts-like square-root
          response of salt inventory to upstream pressure, while a system with
          $\Bra \ll 1$ presents $n \rightarrow 2$ and looks Henry-like. The
          pressure exponent measured in a permeation experiment is therefore a
          \emph{readout of the branching ratio}, not a fixed property of the
          salt.

    \item This reframes an apparent inconsistency in the literature as a physical
          result. The FLiBe and FLiNaK datasets need not disagree: they may
          simply have been taken at different redox states, hence at different
          $\Bra$, hence with different apparent exponents. Present this as a
          falsifiable prediction --- \emph{a redox sweep at fixed temperature
          should move the measured exponent continuously between 1 and 2}. That
          is a concrete, cheap experiment for \HYPERION{} and probably the most
          useful single thing this paper can hand the experimentalists.

    \item Honest statement of the weakness this creates for \emph{us}:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item the most-cited Ni/FLiBe permeation dataset cannot be ingested
                    into a transport code without first assuming the speciation
                    that this paper argues is unknown --- circular;
              \item we therefore cannot use \cite{calderoni_measurement_2008} as
                    an independent parameterisation of the salt-side transport
                    properties; we use it only as qualitative evidence that the
                    interface is not a simple recombination boundary;
              \item our own $k_\mathrm{r}$, $k_\mathrm{f}$ are consequently
                    \emph{fitted} to \HYPERION{} transients rather than taken
                    from the literature, and the resulting identifiability
                    question is discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:hyperion_branching};
              \item anywhere we do quote a literature solubility, state the
                    assumed law alongside it.
          \end{itemize}

    \item Broader recommendation for the community (one or two sentences, no
          more, or it reads as lecturing): report the fitted pressure exponent
          and the salt redox state alongside any tabulated solubility, so that
          the number remains usable if the speciation interpretation changes.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{When can LTE still be used?}
\label{sec:when_lte}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}
    \item A practical decision procedure --- this is what practitioners will
          actually cite:
          \begin{enumerate}
              \item Is there a single exchange channel? If not, LTE is
                    unavailable, independent of $\Da$.
              \item Is the metal~$\rightarrow$~liquid species map diagonal
                    (single isotope)? If not, LTE is ill-posed.
              \item If both yes: check $\Da \gg 1$, and check
                    $\phi/(k^{+}c) \ll 1$.
          \end{enumerate}
    \item \KEY{Emphasise the ordering: the structural questions come first.} The
          literature has focused on step 3 and skipped 1 and 2.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Implications for tritium accountancy and blanket design}
\label{sec:implications}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}
    \item Speciation controls where tritium goes: HF/TF is highly soluble and
          corrosive and stays in the salt; $\mathrm{H_2}$/HT is mobile and
          permeates out. A model that cannot predict the split cannot predict
          either the inventory or the release.
    \item Redox control as a design variable: the branching ratio is something an
          operator can move. Frame this as an actionable result.
    \item Consequence for surrogate experiments: protium- or deuterium-only
          campaigns constrain the channels but not the isotopologue partitioning;
          fractionation must be modelled to extrapolate to T.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}
    \item Interfaces carry no stored inventory here. Interface trapping and the
          codimension-1 formulation needed for it are the companion paper
          \cite{festim_codim1_inprep}; the W/Cu
          \cite{silva-solis_hydrogen_2026} and Be/BeO
          \cite{hodille_molecular_2022} cases belong there.

    \item $a_\mathrm{F}$ treated as a prescribed parameter: no coupled salt redox
          / corrosion model. Natural next step is two-way coupling to a
          thermochemical solver.

    \item \textbf{Parameter provenance.} Because the available FLiBe solubility
          data are reported under an assumed dissolution law
          (Sec.~\ref{sec:units}), the salt-side transport parameters used here
          are fitted rather than independently measured. The framework is
          therefore demonstrated to be \emph{consistent with} the \HYPERION{}
          observations, not validated against an independent parameterisation.
          Say this plainly --- a referee will raise it otherwise, and the honest
          version is not damaging: the whole point is that an independent
          parameterisation does not currently exist.

    \item Interface assumed planar and uniform; no natural convection in the
          salt; no wetting or gas-bubble effects at the free surface.

    \item Rate constants assumed Arrhenius and concentration independent.

    \item The framework is not specific to FLiBe or to fusion: any interface
          where the transported element changes chemical identity on crossing
          (LiPb, oxide layers, permeation barriers, electrolysis, catalysis) has
          the same structure.
\end{itemize}

% ===============================================================
\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% ===============================================================

\begin{itemize}
    \item Formulated a general kinetic interface framework for hydrogen isotope
          transport: reversible reaction channels on the interface obeying mass
          action, with detailed balance fixing rate ratios from thermodynamics.
          Implemented in \FESTIM{}.

    \item LTE is recovered analytically as the fast-kinetics limit of a
          \emph{single} channel; both the Sieverts/Sieverts and Sieverts/Henry
          interface laws in current use are limits of different channels of the
          same framework.

    \item Two dimensionless groups delimit validity: a Damk\"ohler number within
          a channel, and a branching ratio between channels. Large Damk\"ohler is
          necessary but not sufficient.

    \item For the \HYPERION{} Ni/FLiBe system, hydrogen leaving the metal
          partitions kinetically between molecular and fluoride carriers. The
          apparent interfacial law is neither Sieverts nor Henry but drifts
          between them with loading and salt redox state, so no fixed LTE closure
          reproduces the measured behaviour across a redox sweep.

    \item With H and T present, two metal-side species feed five salt-side
          carriers through a bilinear, non-diagonal map. A per-species LTE
          condition is not merely inaccurate but ill-posed. Isotope swamping,
          interfacial fractionation and surface-catalysed scrambling are
          predicted and are measurable.

    \item Flux speciation becomes a direct simulation output, enabling comparison
          with bubbler and mass-spectrometry diagnostics and turning a
          long-standing ambiguity about the Ni/FLiBe interface
          \cite{calderoni_measurement_2008} into an inferable parameter.

    \item Corollary for the property database: the pressure exponent measured in
          a salt permeation experiment is a readout of the interfacial branching
          ratio rather than a fixed property of the salt. This offers a physical
          reconciliation of the conflicting square-root and linear pressure
          dependences reported for fluoride melts, and yields a falsifiable
          prediction --- a redox sweep at fixed temperature should move the
          measured exponent continuously between 1 and 2.
\end{itemize}

\section{The LTE limit}
\label{app:limits}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Model 1} Write $K \equiv k^{+}/k^{-}$, which detailed balance fixes
at $K_{S,B}/K_{S,A}$ by Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_1}). The flux condition
Eq.~(\ref{eq:model1_flux}) can be rearranged as

\begin{equation}
    \Delta \equiv c_A\big|_\Gamma - \frac{c_B\big|_\Gamma}{K}
    = \frac{\phi}{k^{+}} ,
    \label{eq:defect_1}
\end{equation}

where $\Delta$ measures the departure from the LTE condition
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss}), which is $\Delta = 0$. Two things follow immediately.
Taking $k^{+} \rightarrow \infty$ at fixed $K$ sends $\Delta \rightarrow 0$ at
any finite flux, which is the LTE limit. And at finite $k^{+}$ the defect is
proportional to the flux the interface carries, so an interface that is
transmitting is never at equilibrium however fast its kinetics: this is the
statement of Sec.~\ref{sec:ss_vs_eq} in one line. Substituting the steady
two-slab solution of App.~\ref{app:analytical} for $\phi$ gives the size of the
defect,

\begin{equation}
    \Delta = \frac{c_0 - c_L/K}{1 + \Da^{\star}} ,
    \qquad
    \Da^{\star} \equiv k^{+}\left(\frac{L_A}{D_A}
                                + \frac{L_B}{K D_B}\right) ,
    \label{eq:defect_1_explicit}
\end{equation}

with $\Da^{\star}$ the ratio of the total bulk resistance to the interfacial
resistance $1/k^{+}$, that is, the Damk\"ohler number of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:damkohler}) evaluated over both slabs. The relative error on the
flux is

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\phi}{\phi_\mathrm{LTE}}
    = \frac{\Da^{\star}}{1 + \Da^{\star}}
    = 1 - \frac{1}{\Da^{\star}} + \mathcal{O}\!\left(\Da^{\star -2}\right) ,
    \label{eq:model1_convergence}
\end{equation}

so convergence to LTE is monotone and first order in $1/\Da$. This is the rate
verified numerically in Sec.~\ref{sec:lte_limit}.

\paragraph{Model 2} The same argument applies to a channel of higher order,
with the defect measured on the quantity the channel equilibrates rather than on
the concentration itself. Rearranging Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}) and using
Eq.~(\ref{eq:detailed_balance_2}),

\begin{equation}
    \left(\cm{\mathrm{H}}\big|_\Gamma\right)^{2}
    - \frac{K_S^{2}}{K_H}\,\cs{\mathrm{H_2}}\big|_\Gamma
    = \frac{w_\mathrm{rec}}{k_\mathrm{r}^{+}}
    = \frac{\phi}{2\,k_\mathrm{r}^{+}} ,
    \label{eq:defect_2}
\end{equation}

whose vanishing is exactly the Sieverts/Henry condition
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}). Letting $k_\mathrm{r}^{+} \rightarrow \infty$ at fixed
$k_\mathrm{r}^{+}/k_\mathrm{r}^{-}$ therefore recovers LTE, again with a
leading correction linear in the flux and in the reciprocal rate constant. The
difference with Model 1 is that the effective exchange velocity
$2k_\mathrm{r}^{+}\cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma$ now depends on the solution, so
$\Da$ is not an input to the problem but a diagnostic of it, as discussed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler}.

\paragraph{Model 3} Let both channels be fast, $k_\mathrm{r}^{+}$ and
$k_\mathrm{f}^{+} \rightarrow \infty$ at fixed ratios. Each channel then
imposes its own algebraic relation on the trace values,

\begin{equation}
    \cs{\mathrm{H_2}}\big|_\Gamma
        = \frac{K_H}{K_S^{2}}\left(\cm{\mathrm{H}}\big|_\Gamma\right)^{2} ,
    \qquad
    \cs{\mathrm{HF}}\big|_\Gamma
        = \frac{k_\mathrm{f}^{+}}{k_\mathrm{f}^{-}}\,
          a_\mathrm{F}\,\cm{\mathrm{H}}\big|_\Gamma ,
    \label{eq:model3_fast}
\end{equation}

and there are two of them, one per carrier. No single relation of the form of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_ss}) or Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}) is recovered: the total
salt-side hydrogen $2\cs{\mathrm{H_2}} + \cs{\mathrm{HF}}$ is the sum of a
quadratic and a linear term in $\cm{\mathrm{H}}$, and its logarithmic slope is
the intermediate exponent computed in App.~\ref{app:analytical} rather than
either of the two values LTE offers. The partition of the flux is worse still.
Both $w_\mathrm{rec}$ and $w_\mathrm{F}$ remain finite in the limit, since a
fast channel may carry any flux while sitting arbitrarily close to its own
equilibrium, and their ratio is $\Bra$, which is fixed by the forward rate
constants and not by any equilibrium constant. Fast kinetics thus removes the
defect \emph{within} each channel while leaving the split \emph{between} them
undetermined by thermodynamics, which is the assertion of
Sec.~\ref{sec:model3}.

\section{Analytical steady-state solutions}
\label{app:analytical}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

% The Model 1 closed form below is the one implemented in
% scripts/1-first-order-interface/analytical_solution.py
\paragraph{Model 1, two slabs in series} Take $\Omega_A = [0, L_A]$ and
$\Omega_B = [L_A, L_A + L_B]$ with the interface at $x = L_A$, concentrations
$c_0$ and $c_L$ imposed at the outer faces, and no source. At steady state the
profile in each slab is linear and the same flux $\phi$ crosses both, so

\begin{equation}
    c_A\big|_\Gamma = c_0 - \phi R_A ,
    \qquad
    c_B\big|_\Gamma = c_L + \phi R_B ,
    \qquad
    R_A \equiv \frac{L_A}{D_A},\;\; R_B \equiv \frac{L_B}{D_B} .
    \label{eq:two_slab_traces}
\end{equation}

Substituting into Eq.~(\ref{eq:model1_flux}) and solving for $\phi$ gives

\begin{equation}
    \phi = \frac{k^{+}c_0 - k^{-}c_L}{1 + k^{+}R_A + k^{-}R_B}
         = \frac{c_0 - c_L/K}
                {R_A + R_B/K + 1/k^{+}} ,
    \label{eq:model1_analytical}
\end{equation}

with $K = k^{+}/k^{-}$ as in App.~\ref{app:limits}. The second form is the
useful one. The numerator is the difference of the two outer concentrations
expressed on a common scale, and the denominator is a sum of three resistances
in series: the bulk resistance of each slab, and an interfacial resistance
$1/k^{+}$ contributed by the channel. Setting $1/k^{+} = 0$ recovers the LTE
result, so \emph{LTE is the zero-interfacial-resistance limit} of Model 1, and
Eqs.~(\ref{eq:defect_1_explicit}) and~(\ref{eq:model1_convergence}) follow by
substitution. The interfacial concentrations are then given by
Eq.~(\ref{eq:two_slab_traces}), which closes the problem.

% The Model 2 closed form below is the one implemented in
% scripts/2-higher-order-reactions/analytical_solution_model2.py
\paragraph{Model 2, two slabs in series} Take the same geometry with the metal
on $[0, L_\mathrm{m}]$ and the liquid on
$[L_\mathrm{m}, L_\mathrm{m} + L_\mathrm{s}]$, $c_0$ imposed on the metal face
and $c_L$ on the liquid face, and write
$R_\mathrm{m} = L_\mathrm{m}/D^{\mathrm{m}}$ and
$R_\mathrm{s} = L_\mathrm{s}/D^{\mathrm{s}}$. Both profiles are again linear at
steady state, but the two slabs now carry different fluxes: the atomic flux
$2w_\mathrm{rec}$ crosses the metal and the molecular flux $w_\mathrm{rec}$
crosses the liquid, so

\begin{equation}
    \cm{\mathrm{H}}\big|_\Gamma = c_0 - 2w_\mathrm{rec}R_\mathrm{m} ,
    \qquad
    \cs{\mathrm{H_2}}\big|_\Gamma = c_L + w_\mathrm{rec}R_\mathrm{s} .
    \label{eq:two_slab_traces_2}
\end{equation}

Substituting into Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_rate}) gives a quadratic in the rate,

\begin{equation}
    4k_\mathrm{r}^{+}R_\mathrm{m}^{2}\,w_\mathrm{rec}^{2}
    - \left(1 + 4k_\mathrm{r}^{+}c_0R_\mathrm{m}
              + k_\mathrm{r}^{-}R_\mathrm{s}\right)w_\mathrm{rec}
    + \left(k_\mathrm{r}^{+}c_0^{2} - k_\mathrm{r}^{-}c_L\right) = 0 ,
    \label{eq:model2_analytical}
\end{equation}

of which the physical solution is the smaller root, the one that remains finite
as $R_\mathrm{m} \rightarrow 0$. Two differences with Model 1 follow. There is
no factorisation of Eq.~(\ref{eq:model2_analytical}) into a driving force over
a sum of resistances, so the series reading of Fig.~\ref{fig:two_slab}b holds
for Model 2 only after linearisation about an operating point. And the LTE
limit is not obtained by deleting a term: taking
$k_\mathrm{r}^{+},k_\mathrm{r}^{-} \rightarrow \infty$ at fixed
$K = k_\mathrm{r}^{+}/k_\mathrm{r}^{-} = K_H/K_S^{2}$ leaves the quadratic
$4KR_\mathrm{m}^{2}w_\mathrm{rec}^{2}
- (4Kc_0R_\mathrm{m} + R_\mathrm{s})w_\mathrm{rec}
+ (Kc_0^{2} - c_L) = 0$, whose root is the Sieverts/Henry steady state that the
sweep of Sec.~\ref{sec:lte_limit} converges to.

\paragraph{Model 3, apparent exponent} Consider the two competing channels of
Sec.~\ref{sec:model3} at steady state, in the regime where the reverse terms are
small because the salt side is swept, and write $c \equiv \cm{\mathrm{H}}|_\Gamma$.
The atomic flux carried by each channel is

\begin{equation}
    J_\mathrm{R} = 2 w_\mathrm{rec} = 2 k_\mathrm{r}^{+} c^{2} ,
    \qquad
    J_\mathrm{F} = w_\mathrm{F} = k_\mathrm{f}^{+} a_\mathrm{F}\, c ,
    \label{eq:channel_fluxes}
\end{equation}

so that $\Bra = J_\mathrm{F}/J_\mathrm{R}$, in agreement with
Eq.~(\ref{eq:branching}). The total is $J = J_\mathrm{R} + J_\mathrm{F}$, and
since $J_\mathrm{R}$ is quadratic in $c$ and $J_\mathrm{F}$ is linear, the
apparent exponent of Eq.~(\ref{eq:apparent_exponent}) is a weighted mean of the
two orders,

\begin{equation}
    n = \frac{\partial \ln J}{\partial \ln c}
      = \frac{2 J_\mathrm{R} + J_\mathrm{F}}{J_\mathrm{R} + J_\mathrm{F}}
      = \frac{2 + \Bra}{1 + \Bra} .
    \label{eq:n_of_B}
\end{equation}

The exponent falls monotonically from $n = 2$ at $\Bra \rightarrow 0$, where
recombination carries everything and the Sieverts/Henry closure
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lte_sh}) applies, to $n = 1$ at $\Bra \rightarrow \infty$, where
fluorination does and the closure is linear. It passes through $n = 3/2$ at
$\Bra = 1$. Every value in between is attainable, and since $\Bra$ depends on
$c$ through Eq.~(\ref{eq:branching}), a single interface visits a range of $n$
as its loading changes. Equation~(\ref{eq:n_of_B}) is the analytical backbone of
the regime map of Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler} and of the exponent measurement
proposed in Sec.~\ref{sec:hyperion_branching}.

If the exponent is read off concentrations rather than fluxes, as it is when a
solubility is fitted to a sampled salt inventory, the same expression holds with
$\Bra$ replaced by $\Bra\,(R_\mathrm{HF}/R_\mathrm{H_2})$, where the $R$ are the
downstream transport resistances of the two carriers, since at steady state each
salt-side concentration is its production rate times the resistance it sees. The
two readouts coincide only when the two carriers are transported alike.

\section{Weak form derivations}
\label{app:weak}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The residual blocks are those of Eqs.~(\ref{eq:weak_metal})
and~(\ref{eq:weak_salt}). Their derivatives follow from differentiating the
mass-action rate Eq.~(\ref{eq:mass_action}) with respect to each trace value,

\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial w_r}{\partial \cm{j}}
        &= \nu_{jr}\, k_r^{+}
           \left(\cm{j}\right)^{\nu_{jr}-1}
           \prod_{i \neq j}\left(\cm{i}\right)^{\nu_{ir}}
           \prod_\beta a_\beta^{\lambda_{\beta r}}
    \label{eq:jac_rate_metal}\\
    \frac{\partial w_r}{\partial \cs{\beta}}
        &= -\mu_{\beta r}\, k_r^{-}
           \left(\cs{\beta}\right)^{\mu_{\beta r}-1}
           \prod_{\alpha \neq \beta}\left(\cs{\alpha}\right)^{\mu_{\alpha r}} ,
    \label{eq:jac_rate_salt}
\end{align}

all quantities being evaluated on $\Gamma$. Writing $\hat{c}$ for the trial
function of the species differentiated against, the four families of Jacobian
contributions are

\begin{equation}
    \int_\Gamma \nu_{ir}\,
        \frac{\partial w_r}{\partial c}\, \hat{c}\; v^{\mathrm{m}}_i \,\mathrm{d}S
    \quad\text{and}\quad
    -\int_\Gamma \mu_{\alpha r}\,
        \frac{\partial w_r}{\partial c}\, \hat{c}\; v^{\mathrm{s}}_\alpha \,\mathrm{d}S ,
    \label{eq:jac_blocks}
\end{equation}

with $c$ running over the metal-side and salt-side participants in turn. Three
cases are worth writing out. For Model 1, $\partial w/\partial c_A = k^{+}$ and
$\partial w/\partial c_B = -k^{-}$ are constants, so the blocks are those of a
Robin condition and do not change from one Newton iteration to the next. For the
recombination channel of Model 2, $\nu = 2$ and
$\partial w/\partial \cm{\mathrm{H}} = 2k_\mathrm{r}^{+}\cm{\mathrm{H}}$, so
the diagonal metal block carries $4k_\mathrm{r}^{+}\cm{\mathrm{H}}$ and is
proportional to the interfacial loading, which is the same solution-dependent
exchange velocity that appears in the Damk\"ohler number of
Sec.~\ref{sec:damkohler}. For the mixed isotopologue channel
Eq.~(\ref{eq:iso_HT}),
$\partial w_\mathrm{HT}/\partial \cm{\mathrm{H}} = k^{+}_\mathrm{HT}\cm{\mathrm{T}}$
and symmetrically in T, so the residual of H acquires a block against the trial
function of T. Two species on the \emph{same} side of the interface are thereby
coupled to each other, through the interface, which is the structure that no
per-species algebraic closure can carry.

In the implementation these blocks are not written out by hand: the residual of
each subdomain is differentiated symbolically with respect to every unknown of
the coupled system, so the Jacobian is exact by construction and the sparsity
pattern follows the channels declared. What the implementation does have to
supply is the mapping between the facets of $\Gamma$ in the parent mesh and the
cells of each submesh, since the two restrictions of an interface integral live
on different meshes; this is the role of the entity maps passed at compilation
time, and of the ordering convention that puts \texttt{subdomains[0]} on the
first restriction.

\section{Conservation and consistency checks}
\label{app:conservation}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The zero-flux test asks the kinetic interface to relax to the equilibrium state
when nothing drives it, which is the sharpest available check that the
detailed-balance constants are implemented consistently. We run it on the
isotopologue set of Sec.~\ref{sec:isotopes}, since that is the case where the
statistical bookkeeping is easiest to get wrong. Two mobile species H and T are
held at equal fixed concentrations on the metal side, the liquid has no outlet,
and the three recombination channels
Eqs.~(\ref{eq:iso_HH})--(\ref{eq:iso_TT}) are allowed to equilibrate. At steady
state every channel rate vanishes and we measure $K_\mathrm{exch}$ of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:kexch}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{isotopologue_equilibrium.pdf}
    \caption{Zero-flux test of the isotopologue channels: the three carriers
    relaxing to equilibrium from equal H and T populations on the metal side.
    $\mathrm{H_2}$ and $\mathrm{T_2}$ coincide by symmetry. Left, equal forward
    constants give $1:1:1$ and $K_\mathrm{exch} = 1$; the dotted curve is the
    same case with the mixed channel declared twice, which reaches the same
    equilibrium sooner and illustrates why the duplication cannot encode the
    degeneracy. Right, the statistical factor of Eq.~(\ref{eq:stat_factor})
    gives $1:2:1$ and $K_\mathrm{exch} = 4$.}
    \label{fig:isotopologue}
\end{figure}

With all three forward constants equal the result is
$\mathrm{H_2 : HT : T_2} = 1 : 1 : 1$ and $K_\mathrm{exch} = 1.000000$; with
$k^{+}_\mathrm{HT} = 2k^{+}_\mathrm{HH}$ as prescribed by
Eq.~(\ref{eq:stat_factor}) the result is $1 : 2 : 1$ and
$K_\mathrm{exch} = 4.000000$, the classical value. Declaring the mixed channel
twice, once as $\mathrm{H+T}$ and once as $\mathrm{T+H}$, returns
$K_\mathrm{exch} = 1.000000$, confirming the point made in Sec.~\ref{sec:weak}
that the duplication trick rescales the channel without introducing the
degeneracy: as the dotted curve of Fig.~\ref{fig:isotopologue} shows, it reaches
the same equilibrium sooner. All three cases reproduce their analytical values
to six significant figures.
